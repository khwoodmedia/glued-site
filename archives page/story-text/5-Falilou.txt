9. Falilou
Fueled by Muhammad Ali’s legacy and an unwillingness to accept limits, Falilou moved beyond expectations to build a life driven by ambition, creativity and self-made success.
Growing up in France, there was always an unspoken expectation that children like Falilou should settle for less. Less in school, less in career, less in life. Whenever the news mentioned Black or Muslim communities, it was filled with stigma and racism. While Black people were visible in sports and entertainment, they were rarely seen in executive roles or government positions. So from a young age, Falilou began searching for examples of Black excellence he did not see directly in the world around him. He turned to American culture. The figure he gravitated toward most was Muhammad Ali.
Muhammad Ali’s career represented everything Falilou wanted to embody, even at a young age. He admired Ali’s ability to push people out of their comfort zones and inspire them towards their goals. He watched his fights, his demeanour, his resilience, his interviews. For Muhammad Ali, success was meaningful, but inspiring others to believe they could do better meant even more. Ali fought against segregation, advocated openly for his beliefs, and spoke his mind. He was unapologetic. He excelled to the highest peaks. When someone wronged him, he held them accountable. And he was Black and Muslim.
The tenacity and fervor of Muhammad Ali always resonated in Falilou’s spirit, and he was determined to create a life beyond the limitations others placed on him. He excelled throughout grade school, both academically and in sports. But when it came time for high school, he encountered a barrier that had nothing to do with his abilities.
In France, all students took an exam before entering high school, a test that helped determine their next step. The results mattered, but the final recommendation came from a student’s lead professor. Falilou had performed exceptionally well, both in school and on the exam. He did not want to attend the school closest to him. He set his sights on the largest and most prestigious high school in the area. After acing his exams, he felt confident about the next step, until his professor informed him, along with three or four other Black and Muslim students, that they would not be a good fit.
Falilou, barely thirteen years old, rallied his fellow classmates, all except one who likely feared retaliation or rejection, and challenged the decision. By the fall of the following year, Falilou was attending the most prestigious high school in his area.
He excelled over the next four years in high school, both in sports and academically. The teacher who originally said he wouldn’t be a good fit later went on to apologize.
In his senior year, he sat in math class while conversations buzzed around him about what everyone planned to do next. Falilou, however, felt unsure. Before entering high school he had known exactly what he wanted, but now nothing seemed to spark that same excitement.
As the teacher filled the board with equations, Falilou quietly opened his laptop in the back of the room. He put one headphone in and clicked on Chadwick Boseman’s 2018 Howard University commencement speech on YouTube.
The video was just over thirty minutes long and was celebrating the 150th commencement of the university’s history. The first image that flashed was a black t-shirt that said, “Howard is our Wakanda” in red, yellow, blue, and green writing. Falilou turned on the French subtitles and listened closely as Boseman received the same honorary degree once given to Jackie Robinson and Martin Luther King Jr. As he watched the speech, questions began to swirl in his mind. A Black university? Do those exist? Where is this? He was captivated. He kept watching as math class droned on, completely captivated. It did not take long before he began to imagine what his life could look like if he went to school surrounded by people who looked like him.
He continued the video, sneaking occasional glances at the board to convey mild interest, when something caught his attention early in the speech. Chadwick Boseman described his early days on campus and how an otherwise average day was transformed the moment he crossed paths with Muhammad Ali. Boseman said he walked away feeling lighter, ready to take on the world, and added, “That is the magic of this place. Almost anything can happen here.”
It took only ten minutes and one mention of his childhood hero for Falilou to feel it. In that perfect moment of happenstance, he knew exactly where he needed to go next: Howard University.
Falilou applied to Howard, but he was not accepted because of complications with international students traveling during the pandemic. Still determined to bring himself one step closer, he decided to move to the United States and earn his associate degree at Northern Virginia Community College (Fun fact: Jill Biden was his professor.) After completing his degree, he applied again to Howard, along with schools like NYU, UCLA, and several colleges in the DMV area.
He was accepted to every school he applied to, except Howard. He was devastated. He had come to a new country with one goal in mind, and despite his fierce persistence, his dream suddenly felt out of reach. He decided he would enroll at the school nearest to him, the one with the best financial aid, even as he quietly hoped that something would shift.
On the final day to confirm his enrollment, as Falilou typed in his debit card information, an email from Howard University appeared in his inbox.
He threw his computer across the room and began yelling, screaming, “I got in, I got in.” His aunts and uncles rushed to him, screaming in joy. He was going to Howard.
“Mr. GQ”
His first days on campus were surreal. Everything he had aspired to since being a young boy watching Muhammad Ali in France was coming into fruition. At Howard, he quickly became accustomed to expecting the unexpected. Suddenly, he was at one of the most prestigious Black colleges in the world, brushing shoulders with notable figures and sharing space with people he had long admired through a screen.
Falilou quickly became known for his style and presence on campus. People constantly asked if he was a model. This was not new to him. In high school in Paris, his English teacher had repeatedly suggested he try modeling. She introduced him to a friend in the industry, and Falilou took headshots that were sent to major agencies. They all wanted him, but when he told his parents, they were hesitant. Their perceptions of the modeling world were tied to drugs, partying, and instability. They wanted him to focus on school, so he did.
But here he was in the United States, and the same words were being spoken to him again and again. A photographer reached out to take test shoots, then another, then another. He began posting his photos online, and before he knew it, modeling had seamlessly become a real part of his life. He was excited to show a different side of modeling, one that didn’t center the partying or extracurriculars, but focused on the art.
Around this time, he was sending out his headshots to modeling agencies in New York. It was different from his high school experience, when people were sending his photos for him, practically begging him to take a chance on the biggest agencies in the world. This time, he was ready. But when he sent out his headshots, no one responded, and the few who did were not interested. It was humbling, but ultimately it became a blessing and a redirection. He would remain independent.
Falilou went on to land brand partnerships, leading to features in GQ, and people on campus calling him “GQ man.” At the same time, he aligned himself with the creative community at Howard, joining the Warner Brothers Music program spearheaded by Jasmine Young, who had led million-dollar marketing campaigns for artists like Jay-Z, DMX, and more (this program was also his introduction to the Good Compenny team).
His modeling experience allowed him to hone his styling and creative direction skills. In almost every shoot, he had a heavy hand in producing the vision. He played an integral role in taking small brands he modeled for to major viral moments, which led to athletes and celebrities wearing the same pieces. Like every other part of his life, once Falilou accepted something into his world, he wanted to make it the best it could be, and he quickly became masterful at curating his ideas.
Using his growing reputation as “Mr. GQ,” he ran for several roles on campus, creating full marketing campaigns that included merchandise, influencers, and even celebrities he had met through his modeling work. He brought them to campus to support his campaign and hand out free clothing to students. He was selected as one of twelve Warner Brothers Fellows, a scholarship awarded to exemplary students who received specialized coaching, curriculum, and mentorship. Whatever Falilou touched turned into excellence, but he would not have it any other way.
When senior year approached, people were quick to ask what he planned to do for his senior pictures, a tradition meant to commemorate your time on campus and flex your creativity. Expectations were already high, and he felt nervous, but he knew he had to rise to the occasion. He assembled a team, including a photographer and a creative director, and he styled the shoot himself.
“I want a bison,” Falilou said.
“A bison?” His team looked at him, confused.
Falilou started calling farms to find one, only to learn there were no bison in the DMV. At least, not alive. To avoid losing time, they shot the photos pretending a bison was there and planned to figure it out in post-production. Months went by as they tried to superimpose a bison into the images. Dozens of attempts with AI and Photoshop failed. Nothing looked right.
Then Falilou made a random connection with a digital effects expert from Senegal, his home country, someone who would eventually bring the bison to life. The final photos were so iconic that Howard University shared them, creating yet another viral Falilou moment.
Falilou wrapped up his senior year at Howard in the spring of 2025 and went straight to New York, already carrying a wealth of connections who had been patiently waiting for his arrival. He attended his first fashion week, walking seven shows in a matter of days and closing out Calvin Klein without ever auditioning for the role. Now he was in the city to see what he could create. His expectations were nothing short of the top. He wanted Model of the Year. He wanted to lead some of the biggest campaigns in the world. Fresh off releasing his Vogue feature, he was only getting started.
The same fire he carried as a young boy in France still guided him, driven by a deep belief in himself and an unrelenting commitment to excellence. And much like Muhammad Ali, he did not wait for opportunities. He made them.
