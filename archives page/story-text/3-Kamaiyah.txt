Story 3: Kamaiyah & QuakeBeatz: Ms Everything
Kamaiyah’s album, Ms. Everything, opens with a smooth introduction that is impossible to misinterpret: Kamaiyah is a go-getting, bar-spitting, fashion-forward, Oakland-raised, self-made millionaire. The intro alone allows listeners to step into her unapologetic world.
The album, produced by QuakeBeatz, features 12 tracks where Quake lays down a smooth road for Kamaiyah’s sharp and assured lyrics.
She tells her story of Bay Area luxury—satin sheets, leather coats, Beamers and Bentaygas, courtside seats, and gold jewelry. Parallel to the glamour are honest conversations about mental health, loss, grief, depression, and the resilience required to thrive both in the music industry and in the Bay.
Whether you’re searching for a pick-me-up or a celebratory anthem, Ms. Everything delivers. It’s the perfect soundtrack to start your night or end a long day. It’s a reminder to indulge in life’s pleasures, big and small, because joy is worth savoring in a world that often demands resilience.
“I feel like to be my fan, they obviously have some type of self worth, self love and confidence about themselves, because that’s what attracts you to me,” said Kamaiyah.
I met up with Kamaiyah in an Alameda photo studio in November to watch her shoot the cover of Ms. Everything with QuakeBeatz. The two were coordinated: both wore black pants, Timbs, white leather jackets, primary-colored Tommy Bogo beanies, and sunglasses. Kamaiyah had on a large chain, stacked bracelets, gold cuffs on her braids, and large gold rings on her fingers.
Every time I saw Kamaiyah, she was typically with the same close-knit group: Quake; Tressie, her makeup artist; LinkUp, her producer, and his wife, Tati; Slimm, her manager; and maybe one or two other people.
That day, she was joined by Slimm, LinkUp, Quake, and a makeup artist (though not her regular one). LinkUp, Kamaiyah’s friend since high school, was the one who originally introduced her to Quake in 2022. During their first studio session, Quake didn’t hesitate to tell Kamaiyah when something didn’t work.
“I’m like, ‘Who the fuck is this?’” Kamaiyah said.
“But it just made me fuck with him because it’s like, Oh, you got your own opinion. You’re a free thinker,” she said.
The two have been locked in ever since.
The cover was being shot by Sydney Welch, whose eye for detail shined as she fixed stray hairs or caught a bead of sweat. While Kamaiyah and Quake posed for photos, Slimm texted a fan on Kamaiyah’s behalf. The fan had commented under a picture of Kamaiyah holding a Coach bear, saying she wanted it, to which Kamaiyah replied, “It’s yours!”
Twenty-year-old Andrea drove from San Leandro to the studio in Alameda. When she arrived, she hesitated at the door, unsure if she should walk in, until Kamaiyah invited her inside. Kamaiyah gave her a hug and handed her the teddy bear. The two snapped a picture together. I followed up with Andrea for a quick conversation. She shared that Kamaiyah’s A Good Night in the Ghetto reminded her of her sister, who would blast the album, and how she would hear it blaring through the walls as her sister got ready for a good night in the ghetto.
“I’m a youngin, like, really trying to get it and hustle and listening to her music makes me feel like I could do it regardless of my circumstances,” said Andrea.
Kamaiyah’s star quality showed in her ability to connect with her fans and make them feel seen. I witnessed it when she performed for a 30-person crowd on top of a small café on Lake Merritt. We witnessed it again when she performed in front of a sold-out crowd with LaRussell at New Parish. Regardless of the size, Kamaiyah performed with precision and strong delivery. Over the five or six times I saw her throughout 2024, she never faltered.
Kamaiyah’s 2016 hit A Good Ghetto Night in the Hood, with tracks like Out the Bottle and Fuck It Up, secured her place in Bay Area party culture, but with this album, she set out to do something different. To do so, she returned to the Bay Area’s laid-back, hard-hitting sound. The whole album moved like a night drive through the Bay with no traffic.
“I feel like there’s no constriction on what I can create. So I need to start playing in that realm, like, you know, every project should sound different and have a different feeling,” Kamaiyah said.
Completely independent, Kamaiyah has big plans for 2025, but she said that if she happens to regress—and not progress as she anticipated—she’s throwing the towel in. I highly doubt that will happen. Whatever she does next, the message is clear: Kamaiyah’s legacy is here to stay.
