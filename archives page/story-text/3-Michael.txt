Story 4: Michael Prince
With a rare free day, Michael packs his backpack for a trip to the San Luis Reservoir. Inside, he carefully places everything he needs: a fishing rod, bait, camera, tripod, laptop, and his violin. The man-made lake is filled with fish, including salmon, striped bass and largemouth black. Setting off down I-5 South toward Merced County, he looks forward to the quiet shores awaiting him.
The air at the reservoir is crisp but comfortable, requiring little more than a hoodie. After a short walk through grass budding with spring life, he finds the perfect spot. Michael unpacks his fishing rod, baits the hook, and casts his line into the water, keeping an eye out for any movement. Next, he sets up his camera and tripod before reaching for his violin.
Usually, Michael would create a quick five-minute beat in his home studio, improvising and layering string recordings to build his sound, but today he was also doing a live recording of his song Finesse, released a few weeks earlier on April 19th. The song begins with a haunting elegance, transforming into a deep, yearning beat that somehow manages to slap—making describing it difficult.
Michael returns back to Vallejo with no fish, but a ton of new content to schedule over the coming weeks and months. Among the footage is the live recording of Finesse, ready to be shared with the world.
At first, the song gains traction slowly, finding its rhythm on TikTok. Then, it happens—Finesse blows up, Captivating everyone from car enthusiasts to the makeup and beauty industry, to celebrities and influencers. The ultimate stamp of approval? Its own viral dance. The song soared to chart in Apple Music's top sounds, rubbing shoulders with Mariah Carey's All I Want for Christmas, and amassed over a billion plays in less than six months.
While Finesse has been a massive success, Michael's career is still in its early stages. Without a lawyer to protect his intellectual property or handle publishing, many have profited off his work without proper compensation. Even well-known athletes have screen-recorded his beats, rapped over them, and then asked for his approval after the fact. Altogether, he’s only grossed a few thousand dollars and most of his money from streaming goes right back into Hood Orchestra.
“It's weird because I really have no idea about the music business, besides what I've learned from LaRussell,” Michael explains. “I assume it's big, but the other producer, Koncept, he has a lawyer and people collecting his publishing. I don't even have mine registered. For streaming, it’s maybe $2,000, but I feel like if I were doing everything correctly, it should be much more.”
Still, Michael remains grateful for the momentum. “This whole situation is like, I just have to be grateful that I just have something going crazy and just use whatever, like publicity and stuff to capitalize outside of the song itself.”
Though Michael had been consistently posting original content three times a day for the past two years, Finesse was the track that put him on the map. By December, when he returned to his hometown of the Third Ward in Houston, Texas, for his second annual Hood Orchestra residency, the buzz around his name was undeniable. It wasn't just his Houston community supporting him—the classical music world was paying attention, with whispers that Michael was the next big thing.
This was just the tip of the iceberg for Michael's whirlwind year—a series of seemingly impossible side quests that would only come with pure talent and luck. (I mean he randomly found himself in a New York jazz bar with Jon Batiste and ASAP Rocky, and on another completely random occasion, joined a massive bike ride with J. Cole)
In addition to these chance encounters, Michael released an astounding 49 projects on streaming alone, including albums, singles and live sessions. He's created multiple documentaries throughout the year that he shared on EVEN, fundraised thousands of dollars for music community centers in Houston, and took on roles as a creative director and consultant for organizations to help their fundraising. His year was a masterclass in creativity, hustle, and making the impossible happen.
When I caught up with Michael in mid-December for a follow-up interview—our first since Hood Orchestra had officially become a 501(c)(3)—he greeted me with unexpected news: “I got cut from Beyoncé's halftime show—they don't need strings anymore.” Even being cut from Beyoncé's show felt like a flex, a testament to how far he'd come. Michael's tone was light, almost amused, as if to say, “Even that's part of the journey.”
Michael's mission to bridge the gap for young people in music had only gained momentum. Fresh off a summer of non-stop touring with LaRussell and T-Pain, he was dedicating his rare days off to a packed December in Houston, filled with concerts, student workshops, master classes, jam sessions, and more.
He walked me through his day-to-day since returning home for the Hood Orchestra residency. Michael's roots were steeped in musical history. He revisited his old high school, the High School for the Performing and Visual Arts, whose alumni include Beyoncé, Robert Glasper, and producer Michael Cox. He attended a fundraiser for the late Theola Booker, a historic Black educator and musician. Through it all, he remained focused on expanding access to music education for students. He went back to his elementary school, to teach a class on improvisation.
“I'm starting to feel older,” Michael admits with a laugh. “I told the kids yesterday I made my first song in 2014, and they were like, ‘Oh, that's the year I was born.’” (Born in 2001, Michael is a Leo.)
During college, Michael took music education classes. While he enjoyed the teaching aspect, he struggled to connect with traditional methods of instruction, often recalling how punitive it felt to be a kid with boundless energy and ideas. He wanted to create a space where students could embrace improvisation and lead their own learning—two elements that were largely absent from his own music education.
Hood Orchestra became his way of offering free music lessons without bearing the financial burden himself. Having witnessed the scarcity of music programs in schools firsthand, Michael was determined to make music education accessible to as many students as possible.
As December comes to a close, Michael is wrapping up the Hood Orchestra concert series. General admission tickets for the final concert are completely sold out, with only a few VIP spots remaining. In between performances, he's filming, teaching, creating content, and, of course, fishing. This time, he heads to the lake armed with a Yamamoto bait—the one that always brought him luck back in college in Oklahoma. Michael casts his line and, at last, walks away with a catch.
