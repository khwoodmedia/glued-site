Story 3: CallMeClaydough & Body Music
It’s the first Sunday in July in Vallejo, and the sky is a brilliant, cloudless blue. Clay walks through the back gates and begins setting up the stand for his keyboard, careful with the piece that will fall off if not held correctly. His keyboard is covered in purple and cheetah print stickers with part of his Instagram handle, “Claydough.” He’s dressed in tan cargo shorts and a white T-shirt, complemented by a patchwork bag made from colorful quilts and a multicolored trucker hat.
The cloudless sky is the perfect backdrop for the fourth Pergola of Summer 2024— an event Clay has graced all season. The occasion also celebrates P-Lo and LaRussell’s newest album, “Majorly Independent,” which features Clay alongside artists like Michael Sneed, MALACHI, and more. The green turf in the backyard is marked with a small baseball diamond, and a sign is hung on the fence showcasing the baseball inspired album cover art.
Soon to turn 21 in August, the Oakland native isn’t focused on drinking or smoking; instead, he looks forward to retiring his fake ID at the venues where he performs. The instrumentalist, producer, designer, and creative is on a roll—showing up on more song credits and shows each day. He’s producing alongside industry greats like Hokage Simon, P-Lo, and Mike G, performing with LaRussell, working on a poetry tape with Tietta, building his fashion brand DIZZY, and collaborating with artists nationwide— all while staying true to his musical roots. There’s no sign of him slowing down.
Clay’s journey with Good Compenny began less than a year ago, thanks to @Made.by.Harry. He progressed from attending rehearsals to joining studio sessions to performing on the road.
Clay’s presence is marked by an understated yet undeniable talent. Despite being only twenty, his calm demeanor and distinctive style, characterized by layered patterns and accessories, make a strong impression. “He’s talented but doesn’t flaunt it,” said Keith, Clay’s dad. “I love seeing how he operates in the world.”
On this particular night in May, Clay is at Grill Studios in Emeryville, California, where LaRussell has established a creative hub to push himself out of his comfort zone and embrace more collaborations. Clay, dressed in a blue soccer jersey and a DIZZY beanie, is behind the glass booth, clapping and drumming on his body. LaRussell raps over the steady beat of Clay’s chest, then partway through, switches up the pace. Clay responds by drumming on another part of his body, creating a distinctly different sound as LaRussell continues to rap. Tietta and LaRussell often cheered him on with shouts of “BODY MUSIC!”
Body Music!
Clay’s musical background really begins with his parents, Evie and Keith, two musicians from vastly different parts of the musical world. His mother, Evie, grew up on the East Coast, immersed in clogging, string instruments, and folk music. She went on to form a band, the Stairwell Sisters, which toured all over. His father, Keith, grew up in Waxahachie, Texas, where his passion for drumming and Jazz was born. His dad went on to tour with some of the most famous tap dancers in the world, he joined a circus at one point in his career and in the latter he created the International Body Music Festival, which Evie, Clay and Keith attended as a family.
Clay spent his early years traveling with his parents as they pursued their musical careers around the world. Most of what Clay remembers from this time is through pictures and videos of him at that age.
One video shows Clay at seven years old at the Body Music Festival. He’s standing with two mics in front of him on an empty stage, swaying in front of a crowd of adults. His bangs are close to touching his eyebrows and he’s wearing a white shirt with a car on it. Standing at maybe only four feet and some change, Clay performs to the crowd - first beat boxing, and then drumming on his body, then back beatboxing, but not before a crowd roars, cheers and claps for him.
Fé, a lifelong friend of Clay’s family, traveled with them to the International Body Music Festival. Fé remembers Clay as an open and receptive child, deeply immersed in the diverse musical experiences he encountered around the world.
“Clay was very open, like a big vessel,” Fé said. “He was surrounded by musicians in the U.S., Istanbul, Tenerife, Italy, Paris, and Bali.”
Cecelia, another close family friend and production manager for the International Body Music Festival, called Clay the perfect travel companion. She fondly recalled their first meeting on a warm summer day in the Bay Area and how they later embarked on new adventures together as they traveled internationally for the festival.
“These are people that you know, use their bodies [to make music] and make their living from doing this. So it’s a very, it’s not a huge community, but it’s a very unique community. And these people were the top of their field of what they did. So people from Spain, Greece, Inuit, two Inuit women from northern Canada, France,” said Cecelia.
Along Clay’s travels he was encouraged endlessly to experiment with sound. Whether it was a set of pots and pans and chopsticks, a rolled up flier that would act as a drumstick or his red ukulele, Clay was always encouraged to make noise.
“ It was like a big party, with music workshops, performances, food, and dance.” Clay remembered.
The community quickly warmed to Clay, finding every opportunity to involve him in both the performances and the production.
Clay’s mom described walking through unknown destinations with Clay, trying to not stick out too much, but Clay was quick to say hello to a stranger walking by and would quickly reveal that they were not from there, but as Clay’s dad described, traveling with a kid was like having a secret key - people approached them differently and it established a level of connection you wouldn’t’ get otherwise. Clay was always ready to interact, look people in the eye, shake people’s hands and absorb the information around him.
“He was wired for listening to the variety and musicality of how music can be played, and not just with instruments and voices, but how it happens in nature,” said Fé.
Clay’s mom, Evie, credited Clay’s dad, Keith, for developing his deep listening skills. Keith was always fascinated in the ability of the ear to tune out certain noises - to control all the chaos - and simultaneously tune in, like a microscope.
Keith recalled asking Clay as a child, “What do you hear?” To which Clay would respond, “I hear the cars,” or “I hear the wind.” Keith would investigate deeper, “Well what about that airplane that’s way in the sky? Do you hear that low rumble?” or “Do you hear that dog a few blocks away?”Keith described this process as adding a 3D element to the sound around him.
“He would stand in a hallway that was very resonant,” said Keith
“And either clap or go mm-mm-mm so he could hear the reverb in and he’d look at me and say, ‘it’s working!”
It was fascinating to watch the world through Clay’s.
“He will listen not only to the sound, but he can [hear] intuition, the meaning behind,” said Fe.
Keith’s dad emphasized that traveling didn’t mean vacation, they had busy schedules and spent time in people’s homes, with their families, and were often off the touristy path. With that, Clay was surrounded by a lot of different cultures, many of which the common language that was shared was music.
The foundation of traveling required flexibility, respect, listening, and collaboration skills, of which Clay learned very early on.
“He’s still growing, which just floors all of us who know Clay, that Clay is just still blossoming.” said Fé.
Fé described Clay as a century plant - a plant that normally looks like a regular large agave plant, but every 50-100 years it has the capacity to grow a chute that reaches roughly 10 to 15 feet high. “There will be a blossoming from Clay that will be unstoppable. And that’s what I feel about him.”
As Clay grew older, his parents joked that he quit the band, though he continued to enjoy music. By elementary school, Clay began experimenting with GarageBand on his friend’s iPad. By middle school, he was creating regularly, sharing content with friends, and attending beat camps with his friend Devin who introduced him to Logic Pro. Music production soon became a primary means of Clay’s expression, allowing him to explore various styles.
In high school, Clay transferred to the Oakland School of the Arts, a pivotal moment in his life where he met his main collaborator, Ajai Kasim. Surrounded by like-minded producers, Clay stayed collaborating, until hit during his junior year, forcing him to spend more time at home. All he could do was practice. “That’s when I really got good,” Clay said. “I got ten times better.”
Clay graduated high school in 2021. He knew he didn’t want to go to college, and his parents supported him not doing so. He did a quick stint in L.A. and worked at Trader Joe’s but he quickly realized his creative fuel was drained by the end of a long work day. He returned back to the Bay Area and soon after experienced a serious health scare, one that had the potential to end fatally.
As Clay’s health faltered he was forced to accept a new pace - one where he had to mainly stay inside, rest and not much more. He couldn’t make music - the bass and frequencies would cause him to feel terrible. This lasted for over a year, and he remembers the transition phase of slowly reintegrating as extremely difficult and depressing. Over time, he slowly began to see people again and went outside. Surprisingly enough - one of the first things he did was see LaRussell at New Parish. It was his first time he had seen him live.
By late 2022 and early 2023, Clay was back to creating music with renewed passion. He joined jam sessions, performed with Seiji Oda, Tia NoMore, Ajai Kasim, and others.
In late 2022, Harry (@HarryMadeThisBeat) invited him to a LaHearsal and told him to bring his keyboard. It didn’t take long to realize Clay was a good fit. Soon he was in studio sessions, on the road, and playing at every pergola show.
Keith, now 72, has enjoyed watching Clay’s growth and milestones. The collaborative nature of Clay’s work and the people he brings around remind Keith of his own experiences but in a new era, with Clay making it uniquely his own. Clay continues to expand his musical and creative repotour after a busy year. He produced “BIG DREAMS,” on LaRussell and P-Los Majorly Independent album, he’s been working with Tietta to create a poetry album, he continues to send out beats, loops and packs to artists, played at New York Fashion Week and continues to co-create with artists from the Bay and beyond,
“I love seeing what he’s doing now and how he’s growing as a musician, but also just as a person, as a producer,” said Keith.
“I see him with his friends and the people that come around the house, you know, for sessions and stuff. I really like them. I’m very happy with the people that he surrounds himself with. Really impressed by this generation, and gives me hope.”
A Brief History on Hambone Music
In 1739, during the Stono Rebellion —one of the largest and most significant slave uprisings in the British North American colonies—enslaved people along the Stono River in the Carolinas revolted against their oppressors. (1)
In response, slave owners sought to suppress the rebellion and quell their spirit by taking away the enslaved people’s drums, which were vital to the West African culture from which the enslaved people originated.
To further restrict their freedom, and in response to the revolt, the Negro Act of 1740 was implemented, which imposed limitations on their social interactions, education, food cultivation, and musical expression.
Left without their drums, many enslaved people began to express their creativity and rhythms by drumming on their bodies. This led to the creation of Hambone—a form of body music that remains vibrant and influential. Hambone has since impacted various forms of performance, including tap dance and line stepping, and continues to thrive in many communities today. (2)
Keith’s musical journey allowed him to play with some of the most legendary tap dancers in the world like the Nicholas Brothers, Gregory Hines, Chuck Gree, Steve Condos, Charles “Cookie” Cook, and Charles “Honey” Coles. During a rehearsal early in Keith’s career he experimented with drumming on his body. Both Charles “Honey” Coles and Charles “Cookie” Cook noticed his approach and told him it resembled Hambone music—a style they had performed as children and in Vaudeville. They encouraged him to pursue it, leading Keith to explore and embrace Body Music, a term he uses to describe creating sound with the body.
