Story 3: Legacy. Humble. Myself. Reflection. Malachi's Story
Malachi had always dreamed of performing in the pouring rain, literally. Hit-Boy and LaRussell’s 2024 release for “Rent Due” made this moment a reality. With stormy skies forcing the crowd under the tarp, Malachi stood in a blue and white ombre jacket, black sweats and a black fuzzy beanie, rapping his heart out, all while raindrops made his dream a reality.
They tell me I’m so mature My nigga I’m trynna be I done hurt some people did some things you won’t believe, I wasn’t trying to hurt you I was really trying to grieve…”
It’s a somber moment. The crowd is quiet and attentive. I notice several people with their heads bowed in what I assume was not sadness but rather what felt to be deep reflection. LaRussell stands to the right of Malachi on the bleachers, surrounded by his fans and family. LaRussell’s typical, beaming smile is replaced with a pensive look as he stares into the distance, each raindrop dampening his gray Good Compenny hoodie. As Malachi finishes his verse, LaRussell folds his hands at his chest then opens his arms wide, embracing Malachi with a hug.
From Merced to Vallejo
“Legacy. Humble. Myself. Reflection.” Those are the words LaRussell used when I asked him to describe Malachi, a 17-year-old artist from Merced, with no driver’s license and few months left of high-school. Malachi is most definitely the type of young person that you look at and think, “I didn’t have that much poise at 17-years-old.”
Malachi had a way of carrying himself that you seldom saw in teenage boys, let alone rappers. Malachi was notably the youngest rapper to regularly hang out with Good Compenny. He often came with his uncle, dad and sometimes, his two young brothers. Malachi was relatively quiet, but whenever he spoke, it was always with confidence. Every time he walked into rehearsal, the young artist was laser focused. While many people happily chatted in the corners or cut up with someone they hadn’t seen in a while, Malachi looked to always be studying, whether it was LaRussell’s performance or someone else’s. Malachi was wise beyond his teenage years. LaRussell recalled their impromptu walks, listening intently as Malachi would provide insightful thoughts on music, business and his experiences navigating the world. His emotional intelligence reflected in the way he spoke about taking care of his family, building his faith in God, being a good example for his younger brothers, and his discipline to his craft. Malachi was extremely consistent, something he credited to his dad’s guidance and mentorship.
Though only a teenager, there was no treating Malachi like a rookie. He’s profoundly dedicated to his craft of creating songs that tell stories that are deeply personal to him, songs that he’s learned resonate across generations. LaRussell first noticed Malachi in 2021 after Tietta came across a video of Malachi rapping on a local Bay Area blog and shared it with him. LaRussell was immediately impressed.
“That kid wrote ‘Hood Cry’ at 14. Some of these guys are 40 or 50 and haven’t written anything as great, intimate, or touching,” LaRussell said. “At 14, I was just trying to rap like Lil’ Wayne.”
LaRussell was astounded by how someone so young could infuse more lyricism and emotion into a single song than many do in their entire careers. To LaRussell’s surprise, Malachi crafted his lyrics entirely on his own.
Over the past three years, LaRussell has been dedicated to highlighting Malachi, inviting him to shows whenever possible and bringing him into circles that could open further opportunities. Today, LaRussell continues to mentor Malachi, who in turn teaches people like me—and the rest of the world—valuable insights about ourselves through his music.
Malachi’s hard work is paying off. He ended up on 13 of the 17 songs that LaRussell and Hit-Boy recorded during their brief two-day studio session in Los Angeles—one of which made it on the actual album. LaRussell is currently mentoring Malachi through the process of dropping his first album. His social media following continues to grow and in the past few months, he’s started throwing his own shows.
LA Hit-Boy Session
It was sometime in early January 2024 when Malachi was supposed to be coming to Vallejo just to record a few songs and to show LaRussell new music he was putting together for an album that was slated to come out later in the year. In typical LaRussell fashion, LaRussell asked Malachi if he had an outfit and ID with him (he did) and invited him to come to LA to work with P-LO and Hit-Boy. But because Malachi was still very much a teenager, LaRussell knew he had to call Malachi’s dad. On the phone, they ironed out the details, then, with dad’s blessing, hop on a flight to LA. LaRussell told Malachi before they even got off the plane that every time he sees LaRussell writing, he should start writing too.
Malachi entered the Hit-Boy studio session nervous but left feeling affirmed and inspired.
“Even the first verse I wrote I didn’t know if it was good enough,” Malachi said. “I just went in and I recorded it.”
Malachi recalled expressing his uncertainty about the first verse he wrote during the studio session. LaRussell disagreed.
“Nigga shut up!”
It was clear that Malachi was talented and no one was letting him think otherwise. Malachi wrote another verse. Then another, and another.
“Malachi is only the nigga I know who can write with me, like at that pace,” LaRussell said. “I aint met another artist yet that could go when I go—like I’m usually waiting. That nigga had one where he was done before me!”
LaRussell has no problem telling Malachi this. He sees the young artist’s talent and wants to affirm not just his talent but who he is—Malachi is simply different.
“That just inspired me, it helped me to continue to write even more, because I wasn’t nervous,” Malachi said. “They kept affirming and saying what I was doing was so great.”
When Malachi writes, he thinks about how his faith allows him to feel the presence of God during the process.
“I believe in God, I believe in Jesus and I feel like He be there when I’m writing,” Malachi said. “I feel like I’m not the only person that’s there.”
Malachi left the Hit-Boy session as a featured artist on a majority of the songs that were produced. The Hit-Boy session offered just a brief look at what Malachi has been doing since he was 12-years-old: writing verses. Five years, Malachi admits, isn’t much for people who have been in the rap game for 20, but for him, which he pointed out, that’s nearly a third of his life.
Early Childhood
When Malachi was around 11, he told his dad he wrote something. His dad was expecting a short story but instead, Malachi spit some bars. Instantly, his father, Alex, was impressed.
His dad said Malachi was so good at such a young age, it was nearly impossible not to push him to take it as far as he could.
From that point forward, Malachi’s dad committed to investing in Malachi’s music career. Some days it looks like driving long hours with Malachi’s uncle, Cory, to make sure Malachi could get to where he needed to go in order to access opportunities that would help him succeed and gain exposure. Malachi had both his uncle and his dad’s full support. His uncle would drive and even try to help Malachi run his social media and YouTube to bolster his online presence. Malachi’s dad basically worked as his manager, careful to make sure Malchi was aligned with the right people so that his career could continue to flourish.
This meant driving to Reno, Nevada, Los Angeles and regular trips across the Bay Area for Malachi to work alongside Good Compenny. With Malachi, his uncle and his dad, who Malachi fondly refers to as the “three-piece combo,” were his two younger brothers, both theatrical and full of energy, along for the ride.
Malachi alchemized all the encouragement from his mentors in the industry and family into his music. I’d just seen Malachi perform at the Hit-Boy release party. It seemed like each time I saw him, he was only getting better. On top of that, I heard him sing for the first time.
It’s a Tuesday evening in Vallejo and the sun is just about to set. I’m in the media room at the compound sitting across from Malachi as his uncle and dad watch from the side. The three had taken the hour and half drive from Merced to Vallejo so Malachi could record a podcast and join me for an interview.
I start by telling Malachi how impressed I am, surprised that he can not only rap but sing too, when his dad starts cracking up in the corner.
“What’s going on?” I ask.
“He’s been telling me to sing for ten years,” Malachi said. “Singing expresses things that I can’t say in rapping.”
Malachi’s dad, Alex, has the “I told you so” look on his face, then starts laughing as he tells me how he first found out how his son could sing.
“So the kid’s weird right?” Alex said, smiling as he begins the story.
He continues to tell me about the time when Malachi was in fifth grade, dressed in a button-up shirt and top hat, singing Frank Sinatra’s “My Way.” Alex was taken aback because Malachi was actually hitting the notes. His son could rap too, and write—well. Alex found himself asking the same question LaRussell first asked when he heard Mali: “Who wrote this?” It was Malachi.
Alex continues to witness many proud moments as he watches Malachi’s career grow. From small moments in the house overhearing Malachi rapping his verse from one side of the house as his younger son finishes the verse on the other side of the house, to Malachi finally listening to him and getting the courage to sing, to watching Malachi perform in front of 1,200 people. This moment nearly brought tears to Alex’s eyes, especially seeing everyone’s response to Malachi—his son—as lighters and phones lit up the room, praise and daps following each show.
Malachi is wrapping up his senior year in high school while continuing to work on his music career.
“I had trouble in high school trying to find my like collective, my people,” Malachi said.
Malachi goes on to tell me that he doesn’t have a lot of friends right now— most of them are dead, in juvie or in prison.
“And these are the people that like, aren’t, they aren’t obligated to be around me. They aren’t by default my people, right? But they accepted me,” Malachi said. “They accepted my family and they accepted my little brothers and my fathers and my uncles,” he said.
Malachi constantly reminds us of the next generation and their undeniable qualities—courage, grace, and creativity.
