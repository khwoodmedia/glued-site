3. !llmind
As !llmind steps into his role at EMPIRE, he looks back on a career defined by sound, service and community.
Since the early 2000s, !llmind has built a career as a producer and tastemaker. Along the way, he’s achieved industry-wide milestones: winning a Grammy for a Jay-Z and Beyoncé record, producing on one of Drake’s biggest albums, and contributing to the major motion picture Moana. Yet, as he wraps up his first year in the Bay Area as Empire’s lead A&R, one of his favorite parts has been being surrounded by people who look like him.
“Shout out to my entire Filipino community out here. It is an honor to be around you,” said !llmind.
!llmind first connected with Ghazi Shami, the founder of Empire, and Nima Etminan, Vice President at EMPIRE Distribution, nearly fifteen years ago, when they were just starting Empire. Many of the underground records and songs !llmind was producing in the early 2000s and 2010s were distributed by the company Ghazi worked for at the time. !llmind’s manager of the last fifteen years, Jonathan, was also one of Empire’s earliest employees. Now, nearly two decades later, the experience is coming full circle.
As Senior Vice President of A&R at Empire, his primary role is to “be !llmind.” That means using his skills as a producer and tastemaker, along with his love of bringing people together. The goal is to cultivate a creative ecosystem built to flourish into perpetuity. While !llmind’s success is indisputable, he joined Empire to take that success and share it with as many people as possible. He wants to continue pushing the culture forward and to be of service wherever he can.
He is also tasked with strengthening the connective tissue between Empire Publishing, which includes all the producers and writers signed to the company, and the artists signed to Empire on the distribution side of the label. He gets to play all these roles in Empire’s newly remodeled ten thousand square foot building in San Francisco.
Growing up in a Filipino household, music was always part of !llmind’s home. There was a deep love for karaoke, for singing, and for playing music together. !llmind’s dad played the guitar and was also really into jazz, classic rock, and the greats like Stevie Wonder and Michael Jackson. Every weekend, the house was filled with instruments. His dad would carry the melody on guitar, his uncle would drive the rhythm on drums, and another uncle tied it all together with his vocals. When !llmind was around seven or eight, they would sit him at the drum set, inviting him into the family tradition of creating music together.
It was not long before he became curious about how music was actually made. What did a producer do? What about a singer, an audio engineer? How did they merge all these elements in a studio to create something that felt so complete? And how did the entire process work?
His curiosity only grew as he entered high school and discovered hip-hop. He became fascinated with the producers behind the beats. Without a computer of his own, he would take the train from his home in New Jersey to a record store called Fat Beats, a place filled with soulful vinyl from the seventies and eighties. At the time he was broke, taking on whatever odd jobs he could to build his vinyl collection, and slowly it began to grow.
The first time !llmind made a beat, it was not on studio equipment but on a PlayStation game his girlfriend gifted him: MTV Music Generator. The moment !llmind loaded it up, something in him ignited.
He eventually began adding small pieces to his setup: an Akai S20, then an Ensoniq ASR-10 keyboard, the same model used by many of the producers he admired. Over time he acquired a beat machine and a computer and started learning how to create beats from scratch. He studied the greats, from J Dilla and Dr. Dre to The Neptunes, Timbaland, Mannie Fresh, and DJ Quik. After years of working with that equipment, he moved into Pro Tools, started tracking vocals, collaborated with local rappers, and posted his work on MySpace.
In 2006, !llmind received an unexpected MySpace message from an A&R named Derick Prosper, who said he had been listening to !llmind’s beats. Prosper was working with 50 Cent and had just signed Mobb Deep, during the peak G-Unit era. He invited !llmind to come to New York. !llmind boarded the same train he used to take to Fat Beats to meet with D. Prosper, and he left with a production deal.
!llmind’s beats, made in his mother’s basement using a one-dollar record, were now being rapped over by 50 Cent. He walked out into the streets of New York with his first rack of cash feeling like he had made it. That moment became the motivation to keep going and never turn back.
From there, word began to spread about !llmind. He moved out of his mother’s basement and into Brooklyn, where he started working with local rappers. In 2012 he produced “The Morning” for Kanye West, which was rare at the time since most of Kanye’s producers were signed to GOOD Music. In 2014 he produced the classic “Love Yourz” for J. Cole’s Forest Hills Drive album. In 2015 he produced “You and the Six” for Drake’s If You’re Reading This It’s Too Late. He worked on Khalid’s first album when he was just starting out. He worked with Future. He worked with Travis Scott and Quavo on the Huncho Jack album. He produced for Schoolboy Q, Kendrick Lamar, and the Black Panther album. All of this ultimately led to his first Grammy win for Jay-Z and Beyoncé’s collaborative album, followed by a Latin Grammy for Nathy Peluso’s Best Hip-Hop Song.
“I think the underlying common denominator for me was that as long as I stayed true to myself, kept working, kept connecting with people I resonated with, and continued to provide value, the momentum would keep going. And that is still the journey I am on now.”
!llmind’s discography expanded quickly, and while there were many producers in the field, a few specific qualities made him unique. First, he was not afraid of failure. That did not mean he never failed. He failed often, intentionally, because every misstep brought him closer to where he needed to be. He had zero fear of something not working out.
Second, he loved trying new things. Throughout his career he experimented, tested ideas, deployed new sounds, got roasted sometimes, and kept it moving. Years later, when he was already on to something else, those same sounds he was criticized for became popular. That constant game of hide-and-seek with new creative pockets allowed !llmind to continually expand his artistry.
The final thing that set him apart was his instinct and taste, something impossible to replicate.
After two decades of shaping the sound of modern music, !llmind is not concerned with defining his own legacy. He leaves that for the world to decide. He only hopes that when people look back, they see a man who brought people together, pushed sound forward, and inspired others to believe they could build a world with their own sound too.
