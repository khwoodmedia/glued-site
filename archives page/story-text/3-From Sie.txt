Story 2: From Sierra Leone to Stockton, DJ Kenzo’s Journey Is All About Making People Smile
As a young boy visiting family in Sierra Leone, celebrating life was central to Kenzo’s upbringing. Large, extravagant parties were thrown often—for birthdays, holidays, and weddings. Unlike many other parents, Kenzo’s dad, as he recalled, loved arriving early to every event.
“I wouldn’t just walk into the party and, you know, the night would be made,” Kenzo shared. “I got to see the night slowly build—from eight o’clock all the way to maybe two or three in the morning, or depending on when my parents wanted to leave.”
Kenzo watched in awe as the celebration unfolded—guests arriving, the air filling with the rich aromas of jollof rice, fried plantains, cassava leaves, and akara, and once-empty spaces transforming into vibrant scenes. What fascinated him most was how music had the power to shape the mood and energy of the entire event.
While his cousins ran around or danced, Kenzo found himself drawn to the DJ booth. Standing near the speakers at a celebration, the pounding rhythm vibrating through his chest, Kenzo watched his uncle expertly navigate the turntables, swapping discs and CDs. Kenzo’s eyes moved between his uncle and the crowd, who were dressed in Ashobi—handmade, colorful intricately designed outfits that reflected their tribes, whether Krio or Mende.
These moments planted the seeds of Kenzo’s love for music, long before he built his first setup in Stockton.
Kenzo’s Story
At 13, Kenzo sat in his childhood room in Stockton, surrounded by his makeshift music setup: a DVD player, CD player, speakers, an aux cord, and a splitter cable. The walls were a shrine to his idols—music legends like DMX, Bow Wow, and Fela Kuti; sports heroes such as Marshawn Lynch, LeBron James, and Derrick Rose; and his celebrity crushes: Kelly Rowland, Beyoncé, and Meagan Good (until his religious mother eventually made him take them down which he said he eventually appreciated). His room overflowed with CDs, and though he didn’t have a TV, he repurposed his DVD player as a second CD player. In secret, Kenzo began experimenting with sound, creating his own mixes and mashups—a process that called back to his roots in Sierra Leone.
Kenzo described himself as a lively and expressive kid, full of fun—at least until he felt uncomfortable, at which point he would often retreat into shyness. Above all, he wanted to make the people around him happy, and he quickly realized that music had the power to do just that.
Kenzo continued to experiment with music, making mixtapes and CDs for people throughout high school, including one for a crush. He loved seeing people smile and music was a way to accomplish that. After high school, he began working at house parties and in the college music scene—sweaty, chaotic, and often wildly fun debacles.
He began growing his fan base, working in clubs, and driving Lyft or Uber on the side.
One of the defining moments came in 2019, when he was able to pay his rent and buy his whole family Christmas presents solely from his DJ money—without needing to drive for Lyft or Uber that entire month.
Then COVID hit. Kenzo faced the uncertainty many artists experienced during that time. He moved home, which ended up being a blessing, as he spent time caring for his father, who became ill and unexpectedly passed away during that period. As he grieved his father, Kenzo also felt energized, knowing his father would have wanted him to achieve everything he aspired to accomplish.
Post-pandemic, Kenzo began working for the Stockton Kings’ G-League. This lead to a chance to DJ for the Sacramento Kings. One opportunity after another led to a regular position with the Kings, something his dad had always hoped for him.
“It was sad that my dad wasn’t there to see it, but I knew he was a huge reason why, you know, we were at that level. So yeah, it was pretty crazy,” said Kenzo.
Today, Kenzo is booked and busy. He DJs for the UC Berkeley football team and the Sacramento Kings, has opened for major artists, toured the Philippines, works corporate events for companies like Apple, and is one of the newest members of Good Compenny.
Kenzo and LaRussell had known of each other since 2019 but hadn’t had a chance to work together. Kenzo said that even at that time, he prayed for LaRussell’s success. Of course, he wanted to be part of it, but whether or not that was possible, he wanted LaRussell to succeed. This year was the first time Kenzo and LaRussell really got to work together.
The first time I remember Kenzo performing was at the L.A. residency show. It’s August in Los Angeles County at the SLiC compound, owned by Baron Davis, where LaRussell is holding his first Los Angeles residency series. The stark, clear blue sky and beating sun resemble the backyard in Vallejo, but the graffiti outlining the compound and the palm trees scream Los Angeles. As the crowd piles in, there are familiar faces: Jayla and her family, and, of course, the Good Compenny team—Simon, Splash, Tietta, Coach Ott, Jason, and more.
LaRussell sits in a backroom, while Splash has the video camera rolling (as always), with a DJI drone sitting on the table. Jason snaps pictures. Conversations are typically filled with premonitions of the future. The year isn’t even over yet, and LaRussell continues to hit milestones—sold-out shows, massive brand deals, a country album on the way, and a persistent desire to push the needle.
From the distance, you can hear music starting to get queued up. What began as a mix of background noise—voices, food being cooked, teams running around—suddenly stands out. Splash jumps to attention, yelling expletives and sprinting through the back area, through the first door, and then the next room to make it to the outdoor stage. He leaps onto the platform, shaking his dreads, jumping up and down. The person responsible for the music is Kenzo, who is beaming with pride as LaRussell, Malachi, and Jason walk out to hear for themselves before joining Splash.
With an unpredictable blend of old-school R&B, afrobeats, West Coast rap, and oldies that many 2000s babies wouldn’t know, DJ Kenzo takes us across the map—from the West Coast to Sierra Leone—all in Los Angeles County.
Being a DJ requires being the life of the party before the party even starts. Kenzo is animated, always in great spirits, and curates an experience you can’t find elsewhere. DJing in California comes with its shortcuts; you can easily rely on an arsenal of popular songs that have kept Cali parties moving for the last decade. When Sexy Redd became popular, every DJ used her energetic tracks as an injection of adrenaline when the party was lagging. But Kenzo doesn’t rely on repetitive party songs to keep the energy up. Instead, he focuses on crafting unpredictable mashups that leave you no choice but to get up and dance.
Kenzo has no plans of slowing down, and Good Compenny continues to inspire and motivate him. In 2025, he aims to curate a DJ experience that incorporates live musicians. His aspiration is to become a global DJ—one who sells out stadiums, drawing crowds who come specifically to see him. He also wants to ensure that people recognize the artistry involved in DJing, just as they would with any other artist. Along the way, he’s bringing his infectious energy and the pulse of Sierra Leone.
