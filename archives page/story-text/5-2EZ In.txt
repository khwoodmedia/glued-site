8. 2EZ: In an Era Obsessed With Self-Sufficiency
In an era obsessed with self-sufficiency, producer duo Cam and Tajin offer a counterpoint, showing what becomes possible when two people commit to building a legacy together.
All relationships required trust, but two producers, sharing one board, required a deeper kind, an instinct like knowing your left foot will follow your right. Cam and Tajín, better known as “2EZ,” worked with that same unspoken rhythm, stepping in at the perfect time for each other.
I sat with Cam and Tajín in my Lake Merritt apartment during the summer to talk with them about their standout year, their growing partnership, and what the new year has in store.
Tajín had recently been laid off from his job. It was a welcomed transition, and he greeted it with excitement. He had been waiting for a moment like this, a reason to pour his full energy into music. He moved back home and began dedicating himself completely to his craft. By happenstance, or divinity, this was the exact moment when he and Cam started their fourteen day run of endless studio sessions, back to back at any hour of the day — something that would not have been possible with Tajín’s previous work schedule.
Just based on their Instagram, it was clear the producers were picking up speed. Working with the likes of Tyler Lauren, 3lise, Alien Mac Kitty, Frak, Ozer, Easweh, Nef the Pharaoh, John Mackk. They were just getting started.
Artists loved working with the duo, and that was partly because of the space they carefully crafted. Both Cam and Tajín honored how delicate and vulnerable the process of making music was, and they worked together to build an environment where everyone felt at ease. They often started sessions with long debriefs that sometimes took up the entire time and left with barely any music made.
Artist and collaborator Alien Mac Kitty spoke about the impact of working with 2EZ on her latest project.
“We’ve cried in the studio, we’ve laughed, we’ve screamed. We’ve all had moments of vulnerability together, and with that comes trust and responsibility. That allowed me to open up and give them the same.”
Creating a safe environment allowed people to do their best work. It required a level of emotional intelligence to read when people were feeling uncomfortable, holding back, or frustrated. This was particularly important when working with women, who too often felt unsafe in studio settings.
While in the studio together, the two grounded one another. Making music can be notoriously high-pressure, depending on who is in the room. And more often than not, it wasn’t just the artist and the producer(s); it was also the artist’s friends and team, each with their own opinions. Being a pair navigating the ever-changing landscape of making music meant they always had each other’s back. It also meant that when one started getting in their own way, the other would step in.
“I feel like us together, we are truly better versions of ourselves, which is wholeheartedly important,” said Cam
“We fill in each other’s blanks, it’s super dope” said Tajín
2EZ shared an open-mindedness when they created music. They both honored each other’s ideas and created a process that always prioritized the music over ego or individual pursuits, like two good parents from different backgrounds raising one child.
The Origins of 2EZ
Raised in Berkeley, California, Cam grew up in a household shaped by art. His father, an artist, was part of the Bay Area’s 1970s hippie scene, while his mother, a painter, left the Midwest in her early twenties to find community among like-minded artists in the Bay. Their family home was filled with canvases, watercolors, acrylics, and the sounds of the Grateful Dead, James Brown and Jimi Hendrix.
From an early age Cam gravitated towards music and pulled inspiration not only from his home, but from his school teachers. He fondly remembers his kindergarten teacher introducing his class to Chuck Berry, commonly known as the “father of rock,” and recalls a time when he was engulfed with knowing everything about the electric guitar: how it was invented, the first person to play it, how it became popularized.
In fourth grade, Cam was required to play an instrument in school and he thought to himself: what’s the loudest instrument I can play? He chose the trumpet. He continued playing the trumpet throughout middle school and soon he was playing in jazz ensembles in high school, falling in love with creating music that made people want to dance.
Tajín grew up across the Bay Bridge, in San Francisco, in a home with his grandfather, his great grandmother, his mom, and his auntie. He was the baby, his mother’s only child. He was doted on by his family, and his earliest memories with them, even before any images form, carry a love so deep it brings tears to his eyes.
When Tajín was a child, he would sit with his legs crossed in front of his grandfather’s tape deck. On either side were massive speakers boasting a height nearly equal to his own. He slipped on the over-ear headphones and listened for the whir of the CD player spinning into place or the click of a tape rewinding. The music system allowed him to explore all corners of the world, from Michael Jackson to the Beatles.
Music was an intrinsic thread to the culture Tajín grew up in; his dad played percussion and the bass guitar, his uncle was a drummer, his aunt was a dancer and part of a carnival group. Around seven years old, Tajín began going to the Mission Cultural Center, where his aunt worked, and joined the percussion program, salsa band and theatre class.
At the cultural center, Tajín got to bang on every instrument there was, focusing mainly on instruments from the Afro-Latin world, including: congas, bongo, bells, campana, the repinique and the cuíca. He loved being in community with people, and similarly to Cam, he loved to create music that made people want to dance.
Tajín continued playing music in high school, where he joined the school band. Instead of playing for people dancing, he performed at sports games and school events, and his passion for music continued to grow.
The two first met after high school, when they both joined an Afro-Cuban and Brazilian ensemble at San Francisco State University. Tajín had already been in the group for a year, and growing up in the city, he already had artist friends in his circle. Cam, by contrast, commuted from the East Bay, entering both the group and the San Francisco music scene as a relative newcomer.
“He had such an easy going personality and was just hella adaptable. He was just himself. I think that’s just what made him cool,” said Tajín.
The ensemble, spearheaded by John Calloway, was made up of a dozen or so artists: Tajín on percussion, Cam on trumpet, and others on congas, timbales, trombone, piano, upright bass, and bass guitar.
The group practiced three or four times a week and often hung out long after rehearsals ended. It was in this environment that Cam and Tajín’s friendship began. Post-jam sessions were spent going to viewpoints, smoking, and walking around the city. Gentrification was sweeping through San Francisco, leaving the streets busier and emptier at the same time. The ensemble wandered through neighborhoods, pointing out to Cam the homes that had once been filled with familiar faces and were now occupied by generic techies. The group sat on stoops that technically weren’t theres, wearing shirts adorned with “Bay vs. The World” and poking fun at the gentrifiers who stuck out like bones in broth.
Cam ended up leaving SFSU in 2016, just one academic year later, and transferred to Laney Community College to avoid the long commute and the cost of BART tickets from hopping the gate. He later finished his degree at UC Santa Cruz. Tajín stayed at SFSU to complete his degree, and the two naturally went their separate ways during those school years. Still, they kept up with each other on social media, checking in, cheering each other on, and periodically saying they should cook up.
After graduating, Cam began searching for internships, hoping to break into audio and recording. One evening, he received a call from a friend inviting him to an internship informational session at Secret Studios off Potrero Hill in San Francisco. When he walked into the building the next day, Tajín was already sitting there.
After class the two stayed late, caught up, and finally cooked up like they’d always said they would. Their bond was seamless, their styles blending like watercolor. It was this effortless dynamic that marked the beginning of what would become 2EZ.
The two began getting into the studio more often. Tajín was already comfortable creating beats from scratch with the artist in the room. For Cam, this felt daunting; the pressure of finding a sound that flowed between artist and producer is a delicate dynamic for anyone in the booth, especially someone just starting out. There is no guarantee the end result will be something everyone likes. But when Tajín asked him to join, he said yes. And in 2022, 2EZ became official.
As 2025 wraps up, the duo is ready to keep growing, aiming for global reach and household-name status. Their bond continues to shape the culture, creating space for people to create, grieve, and be seen. And in a culture that doesn’t always make space, they make it look 2EZ.
