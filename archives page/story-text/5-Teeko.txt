7. Teeko
Remembering D’Angelo, honoring turntablism, strengthening the Bay’s roots, and shaping what comes next.
In 2004, Teeko was fresh off a trip to Shibuya Japan, where he and fellow Bay Area DJ’s were invited to design their own turntable. It was a rare chance to customize the sounds a board could hold, and to innovate in an era that was still largely static. He described the starting point like water: he could add anything to it. Teeko worked closely with engineers who did not speak his language to create the turntable of his dreams. A year later, he got the equipment. The result was a melodic, fully expressive instrument that became the Controller One Turntable, released through Vestax Engineering. He returned to his regular spots in San Francisco with his new customized sound.
Years later, in 2008, Teeko was performing at the Elbo Room in the Mission District using his Controller One turntable. His friend, Dojah, filmed the set along with performances from other Bay Area DJs to share with a manager he knew, someone who had previously launched a DJ booking agency and was now working with major artists. The video of Teeko was passed along.
Less than a year later, in 2009, Teeko received a call, the person on the other line was Dom Trenier, who at the time was working with artists like Janet Jackson, Questlove, D’Angelo and Mark Ronson. Trenier was immediately enthusiastic about Teeko’s background and talent. He was willing to do whatever it took to work with him, even saying he would come to Teeko and stay on his couch if that was what it took.
A few days later, Teeko was on the phone with Mark Ronson. Days after that, he was boarding a flight to New York. He had been to New York City before. The crowded streets and yellow taxi cabs were familiar, but as he walked through Manhattan, he knew he was stepping into an entirely new world. Teeko arrived at the major label studio, was greeted by Mark Ronson and soon found himself meeting Q-Tip and working on Ronson’s Record Collection album.
After a surreal few days, Teeko found himself in a cab with Traineire, heading back home and reflecting on everything that had just happened. Traineire praised him for the work he had done and made his intentions clear: “I’m going to get you in the room with D’Angelo,” he said.
In September 2011, Teeko was back in New York, this time in the studio with D’Angelo as he worked on his third and final studio album, Black Messiah, at Electric Lady Studios.
The first few days were spent smoking, breaking down government conspiracy theories, going over Funkadelic and Prince deep cuts, dissecting favorites from Earth, Wind & Fire, and connecting on an intellectual level. No music had been made yet.
After two days of deep dives and chopping it up, Teeko finally stepped into the studio, tasked with laying scratches on multiple projects. First, he worked on turntable melodic scratches for the song “Prayer.” At the time, there was no Pro Tools. Everything was recorded to tape, and you only had one chance to get it right. The process was slow and meticulous between takes. The sound engineer had to rewind the first tape, take it down, load a new one, and reset the machine, leaving about fifteen minutes before anyone could record again.
To pass the time, Teeko hopped onto the grand piano in Electric Lady Studios, right next to Questlove’s drum room. He assumed he was alone, playing bass chords with his left hand, until he felt someone behind him. D’Angelo was standing there. As Teeko played, D’Angelo grabbed a bass guitar, threw it over his shoulder, and jumped in, kicking the bassline. The two started jamming together for a moment that felt like forever and two seconds at the same time.
When they finished, D’Angelo set the bass down, placed both hands on Teeko’s shoulders, and told him he’d be honored to have him in his band. He then sat beside him on the piano bench and taught Teeko how to play “Prayer” on the keys.
(In a later conversation, Traniere explained that the moment had been D’Angelo’s blessing — like a kiss from the godfather.)
Teeko went on to track funky scratches on “Sugar Daddy,” although his scratches on that song never made the final studio album. (Some said the label had done their own cut, one that strayed from D’Angelo’s original vision.)
Teeko left New York floating from inspiration. Trainiere continued to stay in Teeko’s corner, cheering him on and bringing opportunities his way whenever he saw the right fit as Teeko returned to the Bay to continue music.
Years later, Teeko remained in the orbit of D’Angelo and Dom Trenier despite the tragic passing of Trenier in 2016. Once again, Teeko’s friend Dojah connected D’Angelo’s world with Teeko’s, dropping off Jermaine Holmes, D’Angelo’s right hand man, at Teeko’s studio in the spring of 2025 and essentially saying, “You two need to meet.”
“He literally just dropped him off here and left, and was like, ‘You guys have fun playing together.’ He basically dropped off two kids in a little play area,” Teeko recalled.
The two recorded four or five records and instantly hit it off. Teeko didn’t hear from Holmes for a little while, until he called back with difficult news. He told Teeko to keep D’Angelo in his prayers because he was dealing with some health issues. Teeko prayed for him, and the two spent time on the phone reminiscing about their moments with the star, only then realizing they had actually met years earlier at D’Angelo’s comeback show at the House of Blues in Los Angeles, at the beginning of the Black Messiah run.
Holmes was hopeful about bringing Teeko back into the studio with D’Angelo in the near future, excited to share Teeko’s beats with him.
A few months later, Teeko received the call that D’Angelo had passed.
Teeko’s first emotion was pain, pain for the world, for everyone who knew D’Angelo’s music and understood how true, deep, and authentic it was. His second emotion was concern for the future. D’Angelo was a lineage bearer, an oracle of the music’s rich history. Prince, Stevie Wonder, James Brown, and so many others shaped D’Angelo’s fingerprint, and through him, those legacies continued to breathe. He worried about where that history would go with him gone.
Teeko later talked with Simon and Clay about carrying the torch, keeping your soul in the work, and staying original.
With shorter attention spans and the constant pressure to create quickly, there is a real fear that the craft and the time it takes to make great music could become diluted.
The Roots of the Sound
Teeko has played a critical role in connecting newer generations to their roots, honoring the past while encouraging artists to innovate and shape their own distinct sound.
Born in Puerto Rico, some of Teeko’s earliest memories include sitting on the floor of his family home with a turntable placed in front of him. He would place his small fingers on the large disc and slowly pull it counterclockwise before letting it go, then repeated the motion again. He paid attention to each groove on the record and listened for the subtle variations that emerged as he changed the movement and speed. He grew up watching his dad play piano and while he learned how to catch and throw a baseball, he also learned about 12 bar blues.
At six years old, Teeko left Puerto Rico and came to the Bay, where he moved in with his grandparents and his uncle, who had a record distribution company that was putting out gangster rap records from East Palo Alto. His uncle would sit Teeko in the car, barely seven years old, blasting the loudest music he had ever heard, the dashboard buzzing from the bass.
When Teeko was barely old enough, maybe ten or eleven, he worked at his uncle’s Redwood City record store, helping him pull records, move boxes, and price items. At the end of each week, his payment was one record. He pulled Digital Underground, Tupac, and Too $hort. One day, while he was behind the counter and customers were shopping, he stood in front of a quartz-locked turntable. He looked at it irresistibly, like a kid told not to touch the dessert sitting right in front of him. Against his better judgment, he touched the record and pulled it back. The warped, distorted sound caught the attention of everyone in the store, stopping them in their tracks. He knew he had done something wrong, but at the same time, he had captured the entire room’s attention, and in that moment he also knew he had done something right.
As he stepped into adolescence, Teeko became fully engrossed with the turntable and began spinning at high school dances. He soon discovered world turntablism, the art of using turntables to create new sounds, rhythms, and compositions in real time.
After high school, he began participating in competitive DJ’ing and went on to win the USA finals for scratching in 2003, eventually traveling to Germany to represent the United States in turntablism.
The discipline of turntablism wove itself into Teeko’s earliest steps as a producer. Its pillars became his compass. Originality was paramount. Borrowing someone else’s style was a cultural flag, and something people didn’t let slide. The fingerprints of his turntable years still live in the way he slices beats and reshapes rhythm. And now, he says he is finally chiseling out the kind of signature sound that defined the turntablists he grew up idolizing.
Most recently, Teeko has brought his decades of musical experience to Empire as one of their signed producers, signing in February of 2025. Now Teeko is standing at the crux of a vital moment in the Bay Area. He grew up watching the region’s music scene take shape, and today he is witnessing it evolve into something new: the Bay Area renaissance. The Bay has long set the tone for the musical diaspora in this country, but its influence has often been tethered to the geography. This next era is different. A growing lineup of heavy hitters share the same mission: to take the Bay Area global.
Teeko plays a crucial role in that movement. He passes down history, game, and lessons drawn from his own journey, while staying a student of the craft and continuing to shape his own sound. He serves as a mentor to many, whether it is Yee Section members Clay and Simon or the duo 2EZ. He is a fountain of knowledge, continually pouring into the next generation of Bay Area artists. And his story is still being written.
