2. Outside Lands
Kenzo leads a prayer over LaRussell moments before Good Compenny and the Co-LLab Choir take the Outside Lands mainstage. The two squat on the carpeted ground, surrounded by artist trailers nestled just offstage. Kenzo places one hand on LaRussell’s shoulder and holds his other hand tight. Their eyes are closed as Kenzo’s words of protection, encouragement, and purpose move through LaRussell and cover the space around them. After a few moments, they stand. LaRussell turns to the team, now gathered around them, and flashes his signature smile.
“Let’s do this.”
A shawl of fog wraps around the shoulders of Golden Gate Park. The blanket of gray is broken by towering redwoods. A clearing down the center allows the view to stretch the length of a football field. The landscape is speckled with people in fur jackets, art installations, and food stands.
On stage, the Co-LLab Choir settles onto the three-tier risers as Yee Section members check their instruments one by one, in-ears dangling from their clothes. Behind the board, Jacob and Donavan adjust levels. It is Saturday, still early in the festival. The set is scheduled for 2:45 p.m., and the crowd is not yet full. The people at the front are the dedicated ones, staking their claim early, securing their spot for the closer.
The team exchanges glances, snaps selfies and dances before the show starts. Disbelief and giddiness wash across the stage. Friends and family are visible despite the massive space.
Kenzo rotates the turntables on his DJ board. Two scratches cut through the air before he lets the record go and drops the first song. More than twenty people onstage erupt, jumping with wide eyes as they trade looks between one another and the audience. Within the first few notes, the crowd, still only about a quarter full, begins to thicken. The patchwork of grass slowly disappears beneath a growing sea of bodies.
LaRussell walks onto the stage in an all-red outfit, a GC racer jersey and a cow-print beanie pinched at the top to form ears. Splash follows close behind.
“Wow,” LaRussell says as he reaches center stage, beaming at the crowd.
He introduces himself, then invites everyone to close their eyes and step into his world. A single, solemn keyboard note cuts through the air. The choir rises.
“We go dumb, we go dumb, we go, we go, we go dumb,” they chant into the mics before the band snaps into “What We Doin.”
The crowd, that was still moments before, breaks into motion. LaRussell’s command of the space is immediate. Hundreds surge toward the stage, pulled in by his energy as the set fully ignites. It’s not long before LaRussell jumps off the stage, Splash and Jason chasing after him like a lead in an action movie. He cuts through the middle of the crowd, his mic cutting with him, surrounded by thousands of people trying to figure out where he is.
Over the next forty-five minutes, LaRussell guides the crowd through his artistic journey, walking the audience through his childhood, growing up hyphy in the Bay, surrounded by love, light, and the talent that first budded in Vallejo before blooming across the world. He also opens the door to deeper truths, revealing the weight of gun violence, single-parent households, and generational trauma through Kage’s story. He ends it where it all began with GT-Coupe, an early cult classic, but not before taking a stop by his major milestones like multiple Hit-Boy albums, P-Lo collaborations and his infamous solo projects.
Throughout the set, LaRussell weaves in the history of hip-hop, sampling classics like “This Is How We Do It,” “Thizz Dance”, and “Tell Me When to Go.” He traces the genre back to its roots of storytelling and resistance, an art form born from Black survival and expression. Even further, the performance becomes an ode to our ancestors who endured unimaginable conditions, whose paths made it possible for tens of thousands of people to gather and bear witness to this moment.
When the choir sings “Wade in the Water,” a hymn born in the nineteenth century, the meaning is layered. The song carries biblical imagery, but it also served as coded instruction during the Underground Railroad, guiding people to move through water to avoid capture. It speaks to divinity, protection, cleansing, faith, and survival. In the middle of Outside Lands, in a crowd that does not reflect much Blackness, the moment stands as a testament to Black culture, its endurance, its impact, and its roots that refuse to disappear — especially in the Bay Area — particularly in San Francisco.
The crowd witnessed more than a performance. It was a continuation of a long lineage of resistance, hope, community, spirituality, and history. In that moment, dozens of lives converged to create something larger than music.
