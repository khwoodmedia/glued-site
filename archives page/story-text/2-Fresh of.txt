Story 5: Fresh off the Gentle Gig Summer Tour, Sydney and Seiji are now planning their next big move - with intention.
The Gentle Gig video shoot took place on a particularly overcast day in the Oakland botanical gardens.
“How’s everyone’s day going?” Seiji asked, dressed in black pants, a black leather Raiders jacket, a white graphic T-shirt with “Free Palestine” written in a serif font, and a visor bucket hat with the top cut out and a yin-yang symbol.
“Peaceful,” responded one person. “Gentle,” chimed another.
Sydney arrived late, smiling and thanking everyone for showing up - while talking on the phone, giving directions to someone looking for the video shoot location. She had freshly done French curls and oversized tortoiseshell glasses perched on her nose. She wore brown sweats that matched her brown and pink New Balances and Hello Kitty phone case.
Sydney was a perfectionist and a multitasker. She ensured everyone knew their roles, always checking over their shoulders. She managed to drop location pins, chat with everyone who arrived, monitor camera angles, snap photos, and guide people back to the shoot. What started as Seiji’s 12-bar freestyle post on Instagram to a xylophone beat by Jake Chapman (@chappymilkshake), “The Gentle Gig” had grown into a full-blown anthem, capturing the attention of millions over the last few months. Now, Seiji was teaming up with Jason Hayes to produce a music video for the song that reflected Seiji’s personal experience in the Bay Area, one that was equal parts hyphy and grounded in nature. The video featured Seiji in the botanical gardens, with two other dancers on either side of him. As water flowed through the rocks, Seiji and the two dancers began to turf, gig, and smeeze in front of lush green trees to Seiji’s soft knocking lyrics.
“A very therapeutic thizz I’m gentle gig general Ya I go dumb but I keep it so minimal”
In the final music video, the botanic visuals were juxtaposed with two cars in a parking lot, drawing circles with screeching rubber tires, as smoke billowed behind.
Sydney sat in my living room in late July to talk to me about Seiji and her partnership, as well own experiences navigating the creative industry. We had just returned from a late birthday dinner for Sydney in Sausalito, and switched from our dresses and skirts to sweats for the interview. I asked her what she pictured for Seiji in the future,
“Everything? Um, I want to see Seiji, like, in his stylish fashion bag. I want to see him walk in Paris Fashion Week. Like, he got that model physique - I don’t know if y’all be seeing him — but, my boy be tall. My boy is tall, He’s handsome. Like he literally can do that, aside from music, right?”
I agreed.
And I could see it happening with Sydney by his side.
In mid-2022, Seiji mentioned on Instagram that he was looking to expand his team, and on a whim, Sydney reached out. Sydney was already a fan of Seiji’s work, and Seiji knew about Sydney’s reputation as a photographer through word of mouth and their shared creative scene. Their initial connection, which began with consistent collaboration and Sydney assisting with social media and marketing, eventually grew into Sydney becoming Seiji’s manager.
According to Sydney, two years ago, things were different for her and Seiji. People who had once ignored them were now reaching out.
“I never forget,” she said, looking at me with a gorgeous grin. And it seemed to be true —especially those who didn’t say happy birthday to her a few weeks prior. Sydney acknowledged Seiji and her were still fairly new to the game, but they’d been around long enough to see how things unfold in the music industry—both beautifully and tragically.
In the last few months, things had really started to ramp up. The Gentle Gig song went very viral and brought Seiji a ton of new fans, including some that Sydney was particularly hyped for, like Jhené Aiko, E-40 and Kehlani. While Sydney was excited - she witnessed first hand all the work Seiji put in up to this moment - she was always extremely cautious about accepting new business ventures and wanted to get things right, the first time. While Seiji said he’s always been comfortable putting things together in whatever way, Bay Area style, out-the-trunk, that’s not Sydney’s approach. She demanded the best, and beyond that, she knew that both she and the artists she worked with deserved it.
Sydney brought a high level of professionalism to everything she did. She managed to strike a crucial balance between being a persistent dreamer and staying acutely aware of the realities— harsh or not—of the path they were forging. Additionally, she was fiercely protective of the people she cared about. She often joked about not wanting kids because between being an aunt and a manger, she felt that was more than enough. Nevertheless, her motherly nature led to her approach situations with a protective instinct, a deeply fortified love, and a relentless attitude to get things done.
As Seiji and Sydney both grew, they were also paying attention to how they were feeling along the way. They agreed, early on, that they wanted to take their time and be intentional.
“Nobody has time to be sued. Like, let’s make music. Let’s do it the right way. Let’s make sure we have a nice plan for the majority of things that we do. And, you know, keep it pushing,” said Sydney.
“You have to rest in order to be present, like you have to not do — to be able to do at your fullest capacity. I think for me, at least, that’s what works for me, is to be able to have time to be alone so that I can fully show up when I’m around people and when I’m performing,” said Seiji.
Sydney had an expert ability to switch between what needed to be done in the short-term, and what she saw as the big picture. She’s been breaking Bay Area records since high school, she graduated from San Francisco State University with a B.A. in Photojournalism, then got a Music Business Marketing and Management degree from Berklee Music College and then just got accepted to be in a concert cohort for UC Theatre.
Seiji began making music in middle school and continued to experiment with his style throughout high school. He remembered being young and creating parodies for family members as birthday presents, including a remix of “Stuntin’ Like My Daddy” by Birdman and Lil Wayne for his stepmom, which he produced using the Audacity music program.
He went to University of California, Los Angeles to study arts, music and business. In 2018, during his sophomore year of college, Seiji and his little brother created a song called “Trapanese.” Seiji made the beat for his brother, Lil Ricefield (@lilricefield), on an hour-long car ride, a parody of their Asian heritage and the many bad jokes that came with it. The song went viral, landing Seiji and his brother an interview on Genius and started getting Seiji noticed in class.
At first, he was unsure of the attention.
“But then I’m like, it’s cool that people recognize me for something that I’m doing. And even if I didn’t feel strongly about that song as like, a representation of me, it made me realize that okay, if I make something that I really like and then people recognize me for that I will want that,” said Seiji.
Seiji was set on creating music that felt to be a more accurate representation of himself, he continued to experiment with his style, but not without a few bumps along the way.
Seiji got in a bad snowboarding accident the following year, which resulted in a broken neck. The injury had forced him to slow down a bit, and with it he — he created a comeback song, “Aero” which was released in May of 2019. This song, he said, marked the birth of his signature sound.
“That song is kind of the first step for me as a solo artist, doing and finding my sound like the Lo Fi hyphy sound. I feel like I was born with that song,” said Seiji.
Aero ended up doing numbers, amassing almost 2 million Spotify plays (to date), and marked the beginning of Seiji diving into his lo-fi hyphy sound. Seiji has received an overwhelming amount of love in response to his music. One woman reached out to let Seiji know that she worked at a lavender farm and played his music when they harvested lavender. However, he noted that one of his favorite responses was when a person described his music as “ya dameaningful.” Seeing music through other people’s eyes, for Seiji, is one of the most rewarding parts of being an artist.
