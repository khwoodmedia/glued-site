10. Rent Paid
LaRussell returns to Los Angeles for his second album with Hit-Boy.
The hallway leading to Hit-Boy’s studio is lined with silver, gold, and platinum plaques, a testament to the decades of talent that have passed through the building. At the end of the hallway is a door with a ring camera attached. As I pass through the door, I’m immediately immersed in Hit-Boy’s legacy. Grammys are casually stacked on top of each other (one adorned with a VIP Baby Shark lanyard), a Racks in the Middle plaque hung prominently on the wall, and shelves showcase everything from a Magic Johnson Pop doll to Bape stuffed animals.
Hit-Boy was having another standout year. His production on Beyoncé’s Texas Hold ’Em hit #1, making her the first Black woman to top the Hot 100 with a country song, according to Billboard. Now, he and LaRussell had joined forces again for Rent Paid, the much-anticipated sequel to their first album, and with it came many unexpected guests.
The studio is packed. Everyone from Good Compenny, Tietta, Jason, LaRussell, Splash, Mike G., Malachi, Michael, and Vince, squeezed into every available corner. At the front, Bosnian engineer Davis Strauss, who also engineered Rent Due, worked behind a large monitor. Hit-Boy floated in and out, in straight-back braids and a plain white T-shirt. To the left of Strauss was LeftyGunPlay.
LaRussell’s process in the studio is always the same: catch the beat, write for a few minutes, 20 at the absolute most, and record the song. Malachi would step in next, with a process very closely parallel to LaRussell’s, sometimes even faster. Michael would go in after and add strings, and the entire track came together nearly seamlessly and pretty quickly.
Now, at Hit-Boy’s studio, the routine was no different. LaRussell laid down his verse in just one or two takes. Malachi followed, replicating his efficiency. Then it was LeftyGunPlay’s turn, shirtless, wearing a blue beanie, covered head to toe in tattoos, and clutching a Styrofoam cup, pen, and yellow pad of paper. Instantly, Lefty brought a chaotic and captivating energy to the mic. Draped in gold chains, he wrote and rewrote his lyrics on the fly, swapping out words as he said them out loud to himself, and chucking them out just as quickly. He was borderline self-deprecating at times, saying to himself, “Nah, that’s not it,” before diving back in, occasionally seeking feedback from his girl friend.
I hadn’t known of LeftyGunPlay before this session (it was months before Kendrick Lamar’s GNX album featuring “TV Off” would dominate the airwaves and the Grammys and climb the charts again). At first, I was hesitant about how his sound would mesh with LaRussell and Malachi’s. Lefty was far from a family-friendly artist, he self identified as a gangbanger. While Malachi talked about cracking crab legs and U.N.K., Lefty’s verses had gunshots in the background and as he spoke candidly about his life in the streets of L.A.
But the first thing that won me over was his avoidance of the N-word (which I had braced for and see too often unquestioningly adopted by non-Black POC communities). This quickly disarmed me.
The next thing that made me a fan was his process. Though chaotic, it was mesmerizing, his voice was a blend of enunciated mumbles and rolling rhythms. His words stacked like uneven, smooth stone towers that people make in rushing rivers. Through trial and error, throwing out and re-recording words and phrases, his lyrics began to find their center, and the verse took shape. He tried things that often left the room laughing. Whatever came to his head, he brought to the room, and nine out of ten times, it was pretty fucking hilarious.
After Lefty recorded his verse, it was time for Michael to add strings. The room fell silent, which was typical when Michael played, it was always deeply moving.
LeftyGunPlay broke the silence with a laugh:
“He’s gonna steal my girl, foo! He’s gonna steal my girl with that violin.”
We all cracked up, but Michael didn’t respond, though there may have been a slight smirk.
I couldn’t help but reflect on the contrast between the session we had just left, when Sam Opoku closed it with a traditional African hymn, and now, creating music with LeftyGunPlay in Hit-Boy’s studio. It was a session born out of pure happenstance, a scheduling overlap that brought LaRussell and Lefty together.
But that’s the beauty of music: unexpected collaborations that just work.
