Story 1: Lakefest Fashion Show
It was the middle of June, and Ariel was wrestling with a decision. She had been contemplating whether to ask her community on Instagram to contribute to a kids’ fashion show she had been asked to organize for the Lakefest Festival. The show was set for the mainstage and was a big opportunity, particularly for the 30 kids who would get the chance to showcase their individual styles. However, Ariel was hesitant to ask for help. She worried about the possibility of failing the kids if donations fell short and felt she would have to swallow her pride a bit. Nevertheless, she reminded herself that it was all for the kids. She decided she would cover whatever wasn’t donated with her own funds—and make it happen for her babies— regardless. Determined to make it work, she posted her CashApp details.
Within a couple of minutes, ping, she got a notification: a $10 donation.
“Wow,” she thought to herself, “That was pretty fast.” A few minutes later, she got another ping and flipped her phone over: a $150 donation.
Ping. Ping. Ping. Ariel was shocked. In a short period of time, she quickly met her $3,000 goal, with her biggest donation being a $500 cash donation. When she realized her CashApp had already surpassed the goal, Ariel began to question it—and then started to feel bad.
“Anxiety through the roof, because I just never want anybody to think that I’m getting over on them, and I always want to be seen as truthful and trustworthy, because I am.” Ariel explained that she quickly deleted the post once she realized she had surpassed the goal.
“I even started questioning, like, why? Mind you, I needed it—I wanted it for the kids. But then I was just like, why are y’all sending me money? Like, why are y’all doing this? It was just like a whole thing within myself,” she went on to explain.
Regardless, Ariel was excited. She said on her dollar, they probably would’ve been going to a thrift store, but she would’ve made it happen. However, with $100 each in their pocket, each kid was able to go to the mall and pick out their outfit—including accessories.
Among the shoppers were Avery and Major, two familiar faces you may recognize from Good Compenny. Major is often seen dancing, laughing, and jumping with LaRussell, Malachi, and Splash, and Avery went viral earlier in the year for her stellar cover of Hyphy 2020. Both Major and Avery’s families had become part of the fabric of the Good Compenny community. Avery was playing pickleball with Tietta and LaRussell when Tietta mentioned that Avery should connect with Ariel and be in the show. Tierra, Major’s mom, was contacted via a note that LaRussell’s mom left on the door after Ariel reached out and asked her to get in contact with Major.
“I was like, I need Major,” said Ariel.
“He’s just so confident, he’s so good at everything, he’s so brave, and he’s just like an innocent kid. He’s just a kid, like, he doesn’t try to be grown, he’s not too prideful, he’s just literally being a kid. And I love that energy. So I was like, I need him,” said Ariel.
Tierra, Major’s mom, was equally happy.
“I was excited for him. He is a kid that loves being involved in things, and he has a big personality, and so I could see that he was nervous at times, but he was really excited,” said Tierra.
Avery and Major carpooled to a park in Emeryville in preparation for the show. Ariel’s friends took time off work to support her along the way. The first rehearsal was shaky; everyone was very scared and nervous. Ariel gave them some homework and had them come back with a bit more confidence. She would also start practice in a circle, with everyone going around and saying an affirmation to themselves.
“It was just so dope to see that, you know, putting some self-esteem into the kids, and having them scream out positive affirmations, just for reassuring themselves,” said Tierra.
After several rehearsals and weeks of preparation, Ariel went with twenty-something kids to the shopping mall. Before they got there, Ariel told the kids to find something that made them feel confident. Ariel was adamant about not helping the kids out too much—this was an opportunity for them to gravitate towards something that would encourage their personal confidence and their own style.
“They could have picked up something I thought was hideous. And I’m like, I know that’s right. I like that. What you gonna wear with it, just because this is how they want to express themselves. This is what they like. And I think I just wanted them to know, to feel confident in their decisions, so they can feel confident when they wear it,” said Ariel.
For Avery, when she was shopping, she wanted to find something that really spoke to her.
“Oh, it was like, it was meant for me, because it was giving, like, cute, but then was also giving, like, the look of my poses, and I was, like, giving a sassy and slay vibe at the same time,” said Avery.
The shopping spree also gave them an opportunity to work in pairs and collaborate on an outfit.
“It was cool because we had a budget, and I was saving money, and my duo was too, because he had extra money,” said Major.
Major and his duo searched for something matching.
“He got a whole fit with a hat, some glasses, some fresh, brand-new Crocs to the socks, and even a little bust-down chain. I won’t say how much it was, because it’s real, but he got him a little chain from one of those little stations at the mall,” Ariel said.
Ariel would occasionally remind parents on the shopping trip to allow their kids to take the lead and remained steadfast in encouraging their individual style.
In less than a month, Ariel found nearly thirty kids to walk in the fashion show, organized several practices, and fundraised over three thousand dollars. The day for the kids to finally walk at Lakefest was fast approaching. Unfortunately, just a few days before the event on Juneteenth, there was a shooting at Lake Merritt, where the kids’ fashion show was supposed to be held later that week.
“What I saw was that Juneteenth was beautiful, and it was a beautiful event, and there were a lot of people out. All I saw was people dancing and having fun and just honestly, a lot of Black joy. So when I heard about what happened, I was just really disappointed,” said Ariel.
Being from Oakland, it made it even harder for Ariel. For a moment, she was fearful that they might call off the fashion show, but she felt confident that the kids would be safe and protected. She knew there was a very small chance of the same thing happening twice. The event was ticketed, with security and police.
“I was like, I trust God. Honestly, that was my biggest thing. I was like, God is not going to allow that to happen with all these babies. And we planned, you know? I was like, I really know that this is going to be great. Everybody’s going to be safe, we’re going to have a good time,” said Ariel.
Lakefest Festival was still set to go on, and on a Saturday afternoon in Oakland, Avery and Major waited anxiously with their duos to take the stage as Malachi opened the show.
“I felt nervous, scared, but at the same time, I was really excited. Yeah, really a lot of emotions were going through,” said Avery.
But once they got on stage, their confidence and practice shined.
Avery walked out in an orange Sublime t-shirt, blue jeans, big hoops with little hearts, a choker with a heart in the middle, a cute bag, and a fuzzy white hat.
“It felt amazing. I felt confident walking. I really loved it. At the end, I was like, we did that, we did so good,” said Avery.
Major and his partner walked out in their matching outfits, complete with their fresh white Crocs.
“I just cooled down, and I noticed a lot of people were smiling whenever I went up there. And my mom was actually excited. I couldn’t hear her because there were so many people clapping and yelling and screaming,” said Major.
It was also an important moment for the parents:
“It was definitely one of my proudest parent moments, seeing her just be so confident exactly as herself, because they were so encouraged to pick their own poses, style themselves, pick their own outfits. It was cool to just see her be so confident in exactly who she is right now. And it just made me feel so good inside and so proud,” said Avery’s mom, Tiana.
After the show, Ariel was bombarded. The kids who were part of the show were already asking for another one, and parents, grandparents, and aunties were all saying the same thing: we need this for our kids! They were half showering her with compliments, and half pleading with her. This was an important moment for Ariel: she had thought about starting a nonprofit but had been hesitant. Why would she need a whole nonprofit for what she was doing?
“God kind of told me then, like, this is why you need to do a nonprofit so more kids can come, so it could be bigger, so you can give more opportunities.”
Ariel laughed, reflecting on a drunk grandmother who asked to speak to Ariel about getting her granddaughter in the program. Ariel told the grandmother to give her a second and then forgot to follow up with her in the midst of the post-fashion show chaos. She tried to walk off, and the woman swiftly stopped her.
“She was like, ah, where you going?” Ariel said.
“I’m still right here,” Ariel responded.
