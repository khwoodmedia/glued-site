6. Alien Mac Kitty
An intimate look at how Mariah transformed lineage and loss into a multiverse of sound, character and survival.
From a young age, Mariah would gaze into the mirror to study herself. She noticed the finest details, the curl of her lashes, the arch of her collarbones, and the ellipses in her eyes. At the same time, she was sharply aware that something deeper was not reflected back. She never felt fully connected to her body. Instead, she felt tethered to the world around her, certain that her spirit existed far beyond this realm.
Mariah was born in San Francisco to her mother Gloria and her father Ronald, more widely known by his rapper name, Cougnut. Both of her parents were artists. Cougnut is a Bay Area legend. Mac Dre would often tell people that Cougnut was his favorite rapper. Mariah recalled that when her father walked into a room, the energy shifted. People lit up in his presence. She also remembered feeling pestered by the constant flow of his fans who rushed up to him, excited and eager to talk, while she stayed protective of him.
Her bond with her dad was different from his relationships with other people. They were always joking, always silly. But she also remembered his rebellious streak and admitted he could be an asshole. Most of all, he was sensitive to energy. He either fucked with you, or he did not.
Mariah’s mother, Gloria, was an artist as well. She was born in Louisiana but moved to San Francisco, where she had Mariah in the 90s. Mariah described her mom as militant, extremely intelligent, and spiritually disciplined. Her mother encouraged prayer, persistence, and affirming the parts of your life you want to keep, alchemizing the energy you wanted to get rid of. Repetition was paramount. She taught Mariah through raw examples, not just instruction, showing her how life could unfold depending on the path she chose.
Both of her parents were go-getters and hustlers, raising Mariah through the nineties and early 2000s. They taught her to be genuine, to be direct, and to never be skeemish. They gave her game in their own ways. Most of all, Mariah was able to see both her parents for who they were. Their imperfections allowed her to mourn them in their entirety when they did pass. They shielded her from some things, yet she still saw them in their full kaleidoscope of colors, and in turn, she loved them exactly as they were.
Now, as she raises her son without the physical presence of her parents, Mariah made a clear choice: she would not shrink or hide any part of who she was. She was eccentric, sensitive, devoted and imperfect. Those very traits would become the foundation of the rich, explosive multiverse she created, a world layered with characters, complexities, stories, and color.
Mariah, also known as AMK or Alien Mac Kitty, is a singer, songwriter, rapper, painter, and explosive creative on a mission to carve out her own legacy. One that honored the hyphy lineage she was born into and also subverted what people expected from Bay Area artists. Her music moved freely across genres. There was her remake of “Toxic” by Britney Spears, an upbeat, raunchy, playful, and chaotic love story. There was her hyphy hype track “GAS ME UP,” an affirmation of self that blended her galactic lyrics with a direct message to anyone who might take offense to her showing up as her most authentic self — a clear demand to get on board or get left behind. And then there was “Passion,” produced by 2EZ, where her sharp, over-the-BS bars glide over soft, melodic vocals.
On stage, AMK was unforgettable. Every performance she felt fully connected to the crowd, and simultaneously in her own world, almost like the wonder of a newborn with no object permanence. All of her attention was locked onto the moment in front of her, yet she occupied a world you could not penetrate. Nothing in her drifted toward yesterday or tomorrow. Every part of her existed on that stage, in that exact moment.
She moved on stage until she was breathless. She shifted from sweeping motions to jumping up and down, to lying on her back with her legs kicked up in the air. Her emotional range was impossible to ignore. Her brows furrowed and pinched when she spoke on something real, her eyes rolled back when she addressed the BS, thizz-faced and whipped her hair when she broke into the hyphy energy that defined her.
Still, it seemed impossible to capture Alien Mac Kitty into words.
Alien Mac Kitty had always been a part of Mariah, for as long as she could remember, even if the name did not appear until later. The little girl who searched for something beyond her reflection in the mirror is the same woman now on stage in reflective white contacts with no pupils, platform reflective boots, long pink braids, and grills screaming “FUCK CHARLES” (a recurring shady character in her art) to the crowd.
The stage name also offered Mariah protection. Many people knew AMK, but far fewer knew Mariah, and that was by design.
“I don’t think everyone should have access to your true, vulnerable self,” said AMK.
Being an artist meant living in a state of exposure, always open to criticism and reaction. There was courage in stepping onstage, but it also invited parasocial attachments and blurred boundaries. AMK became the shield that protected Mariah, the softer, quieter soul beneath it all.
The lines between Mariah and AMK began to blur when she joined the short film “Whatchu Mixed With,” directed by Maya Cameron, in July of 2024. Mariah was cast to play Precious, the big-sister figure in the neighborhood to 14-year-old Alexis, who is navigating life in San Francisco without her father and living with her white mother. The film illuminates many realities: searching for pieces of your identity through community and culture, the importance of kinship when family is absent, and the complex dynamics San Franciscans navigate as they continue to survive in continually testing times.
When AMK was approached about the role, everything in her life felt like it was falling apart. She had just lost her job, she was going through a terrible breakup, and she was living out of a suitcase while trying to stabilize her housing situation. On top of that, filming took place during the anniversary of her mother’s passing. The movie, the team, and the set arrived at the worst and the best possible time. Maya Cameron encouraged her to push through and take the role.
Mariah showed up every day, despite everything working against her. The character Precious was a version of Mariah she was less comfortable with sharing. It reflected the quieter more introverted parts of herself. It reflected Mariah. As she stepped into the role, she realized it wasn’t just Precious’s story she was telling. It revealed parts of herself she had intentionally kept tucked away, pieces that would now be seen on a large screen.
“Mariah is Precious. She is super powerful.”
One of the first things she had to do was change her signature look, the pink accent she had woven into so many of her hairstyles. The moment did not come easy, and for a second, she began to panic. Her hairstylist reminded her that they could always add the pink back in and encouraged her to embrace this moment.
While filming, Mariah had to tuck AMK away, locking her in the closet for the duration of filming. She prayed daily, went to the gym to release excess energy, and stepped away to cry when the weight of everything felt too big, or when the pressure of not wanting to let anyone down became overwhelming. The whole crew surrounded her with love and encouragement, becoming a haven as she worked through the emotions as they surfaced. She tried to stay present, taking each day as it came.
At the same time, she was still performing as AMK, but she realized her energy onstage had shifted because she was so deeply embedded in Precious’ headspace. Those performances would become the place where she poured out all the AMK energy she had been suppressing, leaving it fully on the stage, before she got stuffed back in the closet.
When the night of the premiere finally came, in the middle of June the next year, Mariah finally got to see Precious in her entirety. She described the night of the premiere, at the iconic Lakeshore Movie Theatre, as surreal. She was a natural on screen, her character embodying so much of her own ethos: raw, authentic, nurturing, gamed up. There was Precious. There was Mariah. The role felt like a flawless match and told a story of identity that is often lost in writers’ rooms where diversity and culture are too often left out.
When filming ended, AMK was ready to step back into her bold, unencumbered persona, but what she did not expect was that AMK had drifted much farther than she realized. She almost had to look around the corner to find that part of herself again. Still, she returned stronger than ever, and pieces of her life slowly began to fall into place. She found a beautiful apartment in the city for her and her son, she was on a roll producing and creating music, and she was focused on what came next, which was GIANT.
GIANT
For the past year, AMK had been working with production duo 2EZ to create her latest album, Giant. They were the first producers to truly build Mariah’s sound from scratch, giving space for all the complexities within her to shine.
Mariah has multiple versions of herself, not just Alien Mac Kitty. There is Onyx, Ms. Geraldine, and others, and this project allowed each of those sides to come to the forefront.
And there is one constant across them all:
“I am not the bitch to ever fuck with. All of myself, every last personality. You cross that barrier with me, you gonna see a whole different fucking side of me.”
On previous projects, where she used beats that were sent to her, the music often felt like a reduction of who she was. One person advised her, “You need a sound curated for yourself, because you have a different style, you have a different sound.” Before that, she had been constantly trying to fit into other people’s sonic worlds, ones that did not fully reflect her or apply to her artistry.
2EZ was an opportunity to write a new era.
At first she was nervous, carrying the same hesitation every woman in the industry learns to have when collaborating in a male-dominated space. But soon every session became a blend of therapy and art. They would start by talking about the week, then build the sound from whatever surfaced. No two songs sounded the same. The process was vulnerable, immersive, and capable of holding every version of Mariah.
“I mean, we’ve cried in the studio, we’ve laughed, we’ve screamed, we’ve all had moments of being vulnerable together. There is a real trust and responsibility that comes with that. Because of it, I was able to let myself give them the same vulnerability. It was beautiful,” said AMK.
The project, sculpted over countless studio sessions for nearly two years, has pushed Cam and Tajin of 2EZ just as much as it has pushed AMK.
“I will say this collaboration with her has been the most freeing collaboration we’ve had in the last year, year and a half. It has allowed us to grow in our musical production, our taste, our character, in a big way. The range is huge,” said Cam.
She has learned to set her ego aside, understanding that respect comes first. She listens to 2EZ’s direction and knows that being at her best means recognizing when to be a student and when to lead, when to follow and when not to.
“One reason why we work so well with AMK is because she’s so raw and has so many good ideas, but yet is always”
“It’s unleashed, it’s unhinged, it’s emotional, it’s a roller coaster. It’s everything. It’s energetic. It’s truly a very special thing we all have, and I think it shows in the songs we’ve made,” said Cam.
