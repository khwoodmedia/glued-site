Story 6: Tiny Desk
LaRussell’s Tiny Desk performance, released on November 8, was a watershed moment—not just for him, but for everyone involved.
The morning the performance dropped, my Instagram feed was flooded dozens of clips and countless celebratory posts.
P-Lo was posting about “Give Me a Beat.”
Link-Up was posting about “TEST YA NUTS.”
Vince was showcasing the custom jacket LaRussell wore.
Each artist highlighted the section where they shined.
Ariel shared her styling everyone (except Simon),
and I shared Good Compenny Magazine going up on the wall to join the greats. Even the woman featured in the background of the magazine was posting about it.
I could have gone on. That was the beauty of the moment—a firm example of the kind of impact LaRussell had. He had built a community so deeply interwoven with his journey that his wins felt like our wins. And in many ways, they were. Good Compenny was a win for the Bay Area, for hip-hop, for entrepreneurs, for Black people—and each milestone represented so much more than a single moment.
The iconic Tiny Desk setup exists within an otherwise ordinary space. The NPR building in Washington, D.C., greets visitors with massive electronic news headlines running overhead the elevator entrance. As I made my way upstairs, I noticed most floors were practically empty—rows of cubicles left unattended as many staffers worked from home.
The NPR Music floor resembled a typical office, aside from the obvious star of the show: the Tiny Desk set. Its backdrop—a set of bookshelves adorned with music history—featured treasures like Mac Miller’s hat, Kehlani’s keffiyeh, and countless other artifacts of artistry. There was everything from sticky notes, to flashdrives, to signed setlists.
Everyone from the Good Compenny team was giddy with excitement. As we settled in and the production team adjusted cameras and tested the lighting, LaRussell sat cross-legged, palms pressed together—possibly meditating or praying.
Soon it was time to get everyone into position, the production team worked to squeeze the stacked lineup into the small space. The arrangement stretched left to right: Tione on saxophone, Jimmy Toor on flute (his second Tiny Desk!), Michael Prince on violin, Simon and Claydough on piano, Harry on guitar, Tietta delivering spoken word, Malachi rapping, Shante on vocals, and Splash—well, doing Splash. LaRussell sat front and center on a stool, with mic stand in front of him.
This performance forced a shift in style for LaRussell. Unlike his usual shows, where he improvised and went with how he felt in the moment, this one required a strict setlist and had a time limit. Simon assisted with the production side of it.
During rehearsal, the production crew seemed confused by Splash’s role, which involved shaking his dreads, smeezing, popping collars, ripping off his jacket, and occasionally stepping too far to the left—right in front of Claydough. Glances were exchanged as if to ask whether Splash was really supposed to be in the shot, especially given the space constraints. When it became clear this wasn’t a joke, they asked what his role should be listed as (it ended up being “vocals,” though “irreplaceable hype man” would’ve been more accurate).
After a short rehearsal, the team waited in a small room as a crowd formed around the set. Before the show everyone danced, laughed, and made last-minute outfit changes. It felt like a celebration—a mix of pure love, gratitude, and excitement, the kind you’d find at a baby shower or wedding. Then, it was showtime.
The set opened with Glory/Do That Lil Dance, two unforgettable songs. Do that Lil Dance paid homage to our ancestors with solemn storytelling. Claydough and Simon’s keys set the foundation to set.
“Made it off cotton fields, now we on Tiny Desk,” LaRussell rapped, grounding the moment in a powerful reflection. The song, released in 2022, felt timeless, a eulogy and celebration for the lives unjustly sacrificed for us to be here today.
The performance then shifted quickly into Give Me a Beat, a hyphy anthem and Bay Area love letter. Strong chords carried the energy, matched by sharp notes from Jimmy and Tione’s wind instruments and Michael’s violin, instantly making you want to smeeze and throw on your thizz face.
The set then transitioned into Caught Up in My Head, a soulful country track where Harry’s guitar playing truly shined. LaRussell sang about longing and life on the road, with the crowd responding in snaps and sways.
Before the next song, LaRussell addressed the audience:
“I’m here to keep it [rap] as pure as possible.”
Malachi’s Hood Cry, released in 2021, followed. It’s a story about grief, gun violence, and aspiration. Shante’s silky vocals eased the transition into LaRussell’s Perfect, continuing the themes of survival and generational trauma.
Tietta’s spoken word performance was next—a heartfelt reflection on her journey and a comforting message to herself.
The tempo then picked up with 10s 20s, What We Doin, and Test Ya Nuts. Instruments accelerated, chords grew more intense, wind instruments sharpened, and Michael’s violin plucks became brisk and urgent.
The concert concluded with When I Come Home, a reflection on LaRussell’s early days as a young artist balancing a 9-to-5 job and big dreams. The song seamlessly blended into GT Coupe, a Good Compenny classic representing a different era—smaller concerts, a different team, but the same unshakable goal. The set ended with LaRussell almost whispering the final line:
“Bitch, it ain’t over till it’s over.”
