3. Solano County Fair
I came to the Solano Fairgrounds on Friday afternoon with a heavy heart. It’s the middle of June, and across our screens are horrific images of people being ripped from their families, stripped of due process, and abused by those in power. The sanctioned ICE raids, sweeping across the United States, with a large concentration of media attention on Los Angeles, are a racist attack on every community. They’re part of a larger operation meant to instill fear, convince us we have nothing in common, and scare us into inaction.
But we know the truth. We know the Bay Area wouldn’t be what it is today without immigrants. We know the Bay Area sits on Indigenous land. And we know that we share a vision for the future, one where every child, regardless of identity or homeland, is welcomed into a community that accepts them fully as they are and provides the resources they need to achieve their biggest dreams.
All of this is weighing on my mind as I drive through the Walnut Creek backroads, stuck in 1.5 hours of traffic on the way to the fair. I arrive and park across from the livestock, not to be confused with a petting zoo. The concert is in the same spot as LaRussell’s birthday party last October, except this time the backdrop is filled with pink and yellow carnival rides, their lights flipping on and off behind a spinning Zipper ride.
Vince and his son Liam are setting up the merch table, stretching the cloth across the folding table, only to realize it’s inside out. Liam tells me he’s on summer vacation and staying up until 2 a.m. with his dad. On stage, Geo is tuning her electric harp. To the left, a barn full of goats and pigs echoes with bleats and squeals.
Slowly, the rest of Good Compenny starts to roll in, Clay, Drew Banga, Reece Beats, Jimmy Toor, Simon, Coach Ott, and more. After Vince and Liam finish setting up, they toss a Nerf football across the long lawn. More and more GC kids begin to fill the space, increasing the use, and chaos, of the football game. Sarai is here with Cairo, who just turned six months old. Jacob finds a pair of adult-sized noise-canceling headphones to set over his ears.
By the time the show starts, families have piled in. There’s a grass area in front of the stage and bleachers behind it, but most people stay in the grass. A breeze moves through the crowd as the sun begins to set. Fog pours from the edge of the stage as LaRussell jumps front and center.
His performance captures the crowd immediately. He starts by leaping over the rail into the audience. When he hops back on stage, he asks what people want to hear and pulls up a young kid in a Warriors jersey and GC beanie to sing his favorite song. Throughout the show, LaRussell invites dozens of kids onstage: Avery performs We Be Hyphy, individual kids take turns singing their favorite song, a dozen kids dance together at one point, Blu leading from the front. He brings up kids from E.L.I.T.E. to perform their school anthem that he wrote. One little girl stole the show with her thizz face (the cover of this magazine) and he handed her $100 out of his pocket and offered her unlimited access to future LaRussell shows, she couldn’t have been more than three or four years old.
As kids rejoiced onstage, others did cartwheels in the grass, flipping and stumbling over their own feet as they ran to their moms.
After the show, the team hit the carnival with eight to twelve kids in tow at all times. Tietta was caught between three girls spinning the ride as fast as possible. Avery was convincing everyone to get on the Gravitron, even Clay, who looked unsure about every ride. Occasionally, there were glances that screamed help, but those looks were always eclipsed by the love and admiration each kid had for the members of Good Compenny.
I noticed how many parents felt safe letting their children roam freely across the field. They knew their kids were protected, that multiple sets of eyes were watching over them. I watched the kids on stage being celebrated, having fun. In those moments, no child questioned where the kid next to them was from, what their worth was in this country, or whether they even belonged.
That realization filled me with both despair and hope, because right now, this kind of safety isn’t a reality for all children. And so I prayed: that every child might one day experience what I witnessed that night, a community where they are loved, looked after, and safe.
