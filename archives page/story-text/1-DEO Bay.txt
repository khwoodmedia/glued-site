Story 5: DEO: Bay Area's Hip-Hop Ambassador for Over Twenty Years
In 2001, DEO got hired at 15 — a year before he legally was allowed —to work at the San Francisco Sam Goody record store, after persistently pleading with the manager, Paula, who finally said yes.
Paula was a young Latina woman passionate about music who’d interned at Universal Records when the company still had an office in the Embarcadero.
In the early 2000’s, every type of person came into the store, from teeny boppers and metal heads to elderly classical music lovers and rap fans. The two-level record store in the bustling Westfield Mall featured a movie room, thousands of CDs and tons of records. This was a time when walking into a physical store in search of an album was the typical way of acquiring music. iTunes debuted in January 2001 but streaming and buying music on your phone was not at all the norm then like it is now. DEO started learning about all the different genres of music while working at Sam Goody. At the time, Pop was the #1 charting genre. He sold a lot of Britney Spears, *NSYNC and Backstreet Boys.
DEO’s early days spent selling albums to all types of people reflect what has made him a successful and integral part of the music industry. His dedication to music, regardless of his personal preference, opened doors for him to navigate through different pockets of industry, from formerly managing Lil Kayla, whose demographic is 90% teen girls, to managing Dregs One—a hip-hop historian whose content centers the Bay Area, featuring the stories of OGS who helped shape hip-hop and rap.
Outside of what I’ve seen DEO accomplish professionally, it’s clear that he has a sincere, constant desire to see other people succeed. Whenever I ran into him he was often wearing a baseball cap, tucking away whatever drink he had in hand for a photo, in between conversations or introductions.
For DEO, connecting people is a part of who he is. DEO, now 39, a husband and father to three daughters, has spent nearly his whole life dedicated to the Bay Area. I wanted to learn about how DEO successfully became the Bay Area hip-hop liaison for decades, keen on understanding how he accomplished this while maintaining his signature brand of hospitality and humility. We met up at DEO’s recording studio in San Francisco for a conversation. As I looked around the room, I noticeds a 2x Platinum Lil Dicky plaque on the wall, an Amoeba flag hanging on another wall, near paintings of Mac Dre, with early 2000’s rap magazines piled high on a nearby shelf.
Can you tell me a little bit about what it was like growing up for you in San Francisco?
Homelife was, I mean, without getting too much in the details—it was a typical kid living in the projects. And music was kind of like art, our hub, it was our reason to kind of get out the way and go in a house and do something productive.
What else did you like to do growing up in the city outside of just kind of making music with your friends?
Once I decided to do music full-time which was like junior/senior year, it wasn’t really nothing that was more important than that, honestly.
What would you tell that version of yourself with what you know now?
Learn how to organize your finances better. Learn what it means to start an entity, like an LLC. We had one early but we didn’t really know what it meant. We just did it because that’s what we heard back in like, ‘04-‘05. But learn what that means to do that and learn how to leverage assets. That’s probably too deep. Yeah, find out financial literacy.
Being in an industry for so long and an industry that historically isn’t amazing for your mental health. What lessons have you learned along the way, about taking care of yourself? And remaining consistent?
Yeah, I think that you got to earn those breaks, you got to earn those mental health breaks. That’s probably not the right thing to say. This isn’t a corporate job. If you have to stop for mental health day, every month or every other week, then that’s not gonna work. You have to get to the goal, like finish some goals and reach some goals before you say, I need a break.
What part of being a manager do you think people don’t see?
It’s a thankless job. The manager is like, “the hidden hand.” A good manager is not all in the camera or in the spotlight of the artists.
What’s the first piece of advice you give to new artists?
The first piece of advice I usually give is put music out there. It starts with the music. Like if you’re not putting music out, it’s really nothing to talk about. Like seriously. People want advice before they even see what kind of fans they have or where their fan base is. Some people couldn’t even tell you what their fan looks like.
