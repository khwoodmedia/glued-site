12. Juvenile, LaRussell and Richie Rich at Great America
Rows of green bleachers stretch upward in front of the stage. Parents squeeze in beside their kids, arms loaded with tacos, wings, chopped cheeses, and bottles of water. Towels cover heads. Hats wave in front of faces. Umbrellas pop open. People stand on the bleachers, fanning themselves with one hand while bouncing babies on the other hip.
The stage is wide. Instead of squeezing into corners, there’s enough space for everyone to move comfortably. At the center sits a truck, a foldout table standing on the bed. Kenzo’s DJ board rests on top, his office for the day. To the left stands the traveling pergola, a nod to the origins of GC. Center stage is the choir. To the right is the band.
In the crowd, a sign reading “Thank you, LaRussell” stands out against the cloudless sky. A few rows back, Sarai stands with Cairo, who wears oversized noise-canceling headphones that nearly swallow his head. Off to the right, a roller coaster rises and falls in an endless loop. During the gap between soundcheck and showtime, band members rush to squeeze in rides before the lines grow too long. They return soaked from water rides, shaking out wet hair and pant legs before climbing back onstage.
Backstage, Juvenile waits with his wife and a small crew as Avery pulls him aside for a quick interview. Moments before the show begins, choir member Rain moves through the stage, passing out roses.
Kenzo kicks things off with “Tell Me When to Go,” then flips seamlessly into “She Was a Fairy.” Young kids and Good Compenny members alike raise one leg and throw their arms out, perfectly synced in a TikTok dance. He glides into “Vans” by The Pack, then into “Love” by Keyshia Cole, somehow hitting every generation in a three-minute sweep.
Michael follows, wearing an “I Am Black” bucket hat and a brown hoodie. Phones shoot up instantly, everyone eager to capture whatever magic he brings beyond the music itself.
By the time Juvenile steps onstage, generational lines crack wide open. The older crowd sings every bar back to him, word for word. Parents set their kids down when “Back That Azz Up” hits. Some children retreat further into the crowd as adults push forward, untethered, when Juvenile shouts, “Where my hot girls at!”
Juvenile jumps straight into the three-hundred-person crowd. Children perch on parents’ hips. Teens clutch agua frescas. Parents shield themselves with towels draped over their heads. Hands reach out in every direction. The crowd screams, desperate to touch him.
“Y’all don’t act like this for me,” LaRussell laughs as the crowd, completely enamored, surges toward Juvenile.
LaRussell jumps back in with “When I Get Home,” then slides effortlessly into “GT Coupe,” bending genres without breaking momentum. His crowd control is impeccable as the audience moves from ass shaking to locked in, somber in his words. Juvenile cuts back with “Ghetto Child,” Kenzo scratching underneath while Clay and Simon drive the chords forward.
Richie Rich cuts in with his verse, and for a moment LaRussell, Juvenile, and Richie Rich all sit together on one ledge. It is a rare moment where eras of hip-hop merge, creating an experience that feels just as meaningful to the parents as it does to the kids watching from below.
The show moves forward in an effortless flow of improvisation, with LaRussell directing the energy between everyone onstage. By the end, hair is slicked to faces and babies have settled into their naps despite the noise.
But instead of heading home, the crowd drifts back into the amusement park. It is a chance few people get after middle or high school to move in a group of hundreds, eating together, celebrating, laughing, and letting themselves feel like kids again.
That is the magic of LaRussell. Gathering people like this, especially as adults, feels nearly impossible. Class field trips and group birthday parties fade with age, but moments like this recreate that sense of collective joy on an entirely different scale.
For a few hours, adulthood loosens its grip. Parents dance. Kids run free and indulge in sugary drinks. There is something radical about joy like this. At the end of it all, the truth is simple: people want to gather. They want to celebrate. And sometimes, they just want to be kids again.
