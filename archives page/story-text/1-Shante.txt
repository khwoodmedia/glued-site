Story 2: Shanté: Music & Motherhood
As a child, Shanté’s morning routine began with stepping outside and singing—singing to herself, to the sun and to anyone else who happened to hear the notes flowing out of her grandmother’s East Oakland house. Afternoons were spent listening to the sounds of Charlie Wilson, Marvin Gaye and Oakland’s own Keyshia Cole. In the evenings, Shanté would be playing outside, drawing with chalk while her grandmother cooked, pulling ingredients from the garden.
For as long as Shanté can remember, she’s been asked by her family to sing, even if she didn’t feel like it. Referred to by her family as the “mini Alicia Keys,” particularly by her animal-print-obsessed aunt, Shanté found herself called upon to perform at every family function, from cookouts to birthdays and parties at her family’s ranch.
Years of impromptu performances at her family’s request may explain Shanté’s unenthused look when she walks into the backyard for rehearsals. LaRussell promptly hands her the mic, giving her barely a moment to catch her breath. Begrudgingly, Shanté takes the mic and settles into the bleachers, doing her best to blend into the crowd. No signs of excitement or nerves slip onto the 20-year-old’s face. At 4 ‘11”, Shanté prefers to go unnoticed but once she opens her mouth to sing, you’ll never be able to forget her. Eventually, Shanté pulls out her iPhone 7 to glance at her lyrics, then, with a single note, her angelic, soft, soulful voice draws the crowd into silence.
Somewhere in the crowd, a toddler stands mesmerized. Noel was endlessly curious, the type of child that couldn’t help but make you smile. From birth, Noel was surrounded by art, love, talent, light and endless hands from the Good Compenny crew, ready to pick him up. Michael Prince crouches down to let Noel blindly pull at his violin, Nef the Pharaoh drops to his knees to rap eye level with him, LaRussell holds him in his left arm while he adjusts audio with his right, Tietta catches him when he runs into her arms, pulling his pant leg down to make sure it covers the small part of his exposed ankle.
But the 1-year-old’s deepest connection is with Shanté, his mom. The melodies he sings just like her, the beat that he drums upstairs in the venue as she waits backstage, the turn of his head when she opens her mouth and lets out that first glorious note, the embrace that soothes when no one else’s will. Shanté’s love felt like her voice—angelic, soft and soulful. After rehearsals and performances, Shanté is often showered with compliments. New listeners who didn’t know her before are anxious to tell her how amazing her voice is. Often Shanté blushes and looks down as people gush over her performances.
For Shanté, the 20-year-old songstress from East Oakland, success came quickly. The standout track, “Party on Mars,” from her debut EP Protection, released in 2021, is approaching nearly 2 million plays on Spotify. Just recently, the streaming giant added her to the cover of their Fresh Finds playlist. In February, Shanté opened for Pink Sweats after he pulled up to Vallejo and happened to hear her sing. Her social media continues to grow, with several viral videos boasting views in the hundreds of thousands. Shanté is currently working with the well-rounded and infinitely talented musician and producer, Hokage Simon, to put out new music.
At the same time, Shanté is a new mom and despite quite a few successes, her career is still in its early stages. Each day, she navigates co-parenting, motherhood, love, and relationships. On some days, Shanté embodies the particular brand of exhaustion that only parents—particularly moms—understand.
Until this interview, Shanté and I communicated through side eyes, raised eyebrows, and an occasional side comment at the Pergola. In a moment of irritation, Shanté would say to me,“Did you see this big beefy nigga just try to hit on me?” or, in a moment of doubt, “Is what I said awkward? I feel like it was awkward,” or, in a moment of anxiety, “Do you have weed?” Shanté is only 20.
On a sunny day in late February, I convinced Shanté to come to my apartment for an interview, promising a present upon arrival. It didn’t hurt that it was just her birthday. I was determined to know more about Shanté. She had an energy that lifted hearts but when she wasn’t singing, Shanté seemed like she didn’t have much to say. I wondered who Shanté was at 16, when she first walked into the backyard of LaRussell’s family home.
Backyard Beginnings
In 2020, a friend invited Shanté to sing background vocals for LaRussell at one of Good Compenny’s Live Sessions–a series of short, live performances edited for YouTube. After she finished singing, LaRussell told her how good she was and that she should not be a singing background. They went on to record a live version of Etta James’ “I’d Rather Go Blind.” LaRussell asked if she had a studio space to record anything–she didn’t. Soon after, Shanté pulled up to LaRussell’s home studio and the two recorded her first EP, Protection, in less than three days.
For LaRussell, writing with Shanté was something new and fun. He typically only wrote by himself, but with Shanté, she would hum a bass line for LaRussell or a flow, he’d add his bars, then she’d add her lyrics, going back and forth until they had a complete song.
“It was great. I felt at home,” Shanté said. “I felt like I belonged.”
“She’s my favorite person to write with because it’s so easy,” LaRussell said.
The album easily became an instant success, independent of a major label or big marketing dollars. For LaRussell, this was proof that great music always prevails, because a similar kind of magic happened when he wrote the album “Field Trip’’ for Tessie, an original member of Good Compenny, as well as an artist and longtime friend.

Growing Up Shanté
Before Shanté arrived at my Lake Merritt apartment, I prepared a small surprise for her. I wiped down my wobbly wooden dining room table, whose four legs were each attached by a single screw, then waited for it to dry before placing the black and white striped Sephora bag on the table. I placed a bouquet of roses and peonies for her in a vase, rotating the glass until the best flower stuck out to me. As the final touch, I placed two mini Nothing Bundt Cakes on the left and right sides of the vase.
Shanté showed up around 4:15 p.m., wearing fresh white and pink low top Jordans, Puma basketball shorts with big pink graffiti-style hearts and lettering, a pink Good Compenny t-shirt, and a maroon head scarf. I welcomed her inside, eager to give Shanté her gifts.
Shanté slowly approached the table, examining each item. Weed, check. She tried the Sephora lip gloss in the mirror to see how she felt about the light pink tint. I assured her she could return it if she wanted to. Thankfully, I guessed the right cake flavor. Shanté left my house at 1:30 a.m. We smoked, talking for hours over chicken wings and cake topped with cream cheese frosting on my brown sectional couch covered in fuzzy blankets.
Shanté grew up with her grandmother, her dad, her uncle, her mother (sometimes) and her cousin, Joy. They all lived in East Oakland with her grandmother, who had a massive garden that Shanté ate fresh vegetables from, often playing in the sprinklers and with the rollie pollies crawling around the pavement. She has fond memories of the Oakland of her childhood, an Oakland she recalls as more Black, vibrant, and sunny than it is now.
Shanté described herself as a kid who was very energetic and bright—and of course always singing. She can’t recall a time when singing wasn’t a part of her life. She grew up doing plays with the Black Repertory Group Theatre, a Berkeley-based theater company. She’d beg the same aunt who donned her “mini Alicia Keys” to write down lyrics to songs on a piece of paper so she could practice the song for hours on end. And at family functions, Shanté took center stage.
At the same time, Shanté remembers struggling with ADHD and anxiety as early as elementary school without anyone to support her. Her parents had no idea what anxiety was or how to help her. Her teachers often chopped her ADHD symptoms to attention seeking behavior. In middle school, Shanté began to learn how to manage her anxiety through meditation and other techniques. Shanté said she’d always had a strong relationship with God, and He helped guide her to meditation.
“I was trying to help myself because none of these motherfuckers are trying to help me,” Shanté said.
When Shanté’s cousin Joy called, the door opened for our conversation to become more personal. Until then, Shanté kept details about her family pretty close to her chest, not bothering to go into much detail. When I asked follow-up questions about what it was like growing up in East Oakland, she’d respond with a vague, “You know…” (I didn’t) or shut down the conversation completely. When we were talking about growing up at her grandma’s, she mentioned her cousin Joy. If you ask Shanté, she’ll tell you they never 100% got along, even though they were always together.
“Where is she now?” I asked Shanté.
“I don’t know,” she replied.
I turned to my phone, which was recording the conversation, to ask the same question.
Where you at Joy?
A few minutes later, Joy called. I quickly learned why Shanté wasn’t too keen on going into detail about her childhood earlier in our conversation. A few months ago while Shanté was living with her dad, he held a gun to her head for reasons she’s never been able to make sense of. Her cousin Joy went on to say, “Well you know my dad is even crazier.” Shanté moved out shortly after that, showing up to the compound—where LaRussell and several Good Compenny members live—with nowhere else to go.
Shanté chops her family’s situation up to trauma.
“I had to learn at a young age that you can’t help people who don’t want to change. You can’t want to prove yourself to someone who doesn’t care. I just had to let that go. It still hurts though, but life moves on,” said Shanté.
The urge to escape influenced many of Shanté’s choices growing up. She loved taking the bus almost anywhere. The bus took her to open mics all across the Bay Area. Shanté always seemed to be with a friend or on the go, she preferred to be anywhere but home. Maybe that’s what brought her to LaRussell’s backyard four years ago.
While Shanté’s been through a lot, she seemingly carries the weight of her past, the responsibility of her present and the promise of her future quite well, especially as a 20-year-old mother and singer. Shanté is a bright, positive spirit, filled with faith.
“I can’t escape from music, because I am music. I can’t escape from being bubbly, because I am bubbly, but I still got anxiety a little bit,” Shanté laughed.
I often sit in LaRussell’s parents’ backyard, staring at Shanté as she sings, goosebumps covering my body, a massive smile spreading across my face with pride, like she’s my own kin. Shanté’s music evokes an immense sense of belonging, the type that encourages you to live your truth. Every time we walk past the wooden gate in to LaRussell’s family home, it feels like home. We sing in unison, “We proud, We Black!” swaying or, “This is a family business, ain’t no place like home.” This message, sung from the depths of Shanté’s heart, leaves me feeling settled in my soul, with the hope that—despite the trauma—we come together to create moments where we celebrate one another.
