Story 2: The Reintroduction of Richie Rich
And His Breakdown of Majorly Independent
It’s 2:30 PM at Boss Market Food and Liquor, just down the street from LaRussell’s parents’ house. Richie Rich stands by his caramel classic car, adorned with a black and yellow license plate that reads “7DULCE.” Mike G holds a massive speaker as Malachi rides his bike into the frame of the video, starting his verse. P-Lo and LaRussell are behind him, P-Lo in a black shirt, black Stone Island pants, and Timbs. LaRussell, styled by Ariel, wears a monochrome black and white outfit, including a letterman jacket and a black and white beanie. They’re surrounded by supporters who responded to LaRussell’s Instagram invitation to join the content day for LaRussell and P-Lo’s new album, “Majorly Independent,” which was set to premiere in early July.
The album, a Bay Area classic, showcases the full spectrum of hip-hop, featuring OGs like Too $hort and Richie Rich—renowned for his unforgettable verses on “I Got 5 On It - Remix” and “Ratha Be Ya Nigga” with 2Pac—to new era talents like Malachi, who just graduated high school, and Michael Sneed, whose unique sound continues to impress. The album tells a powerful story of Bay Area pride and community, with each feature and verse strengthening the hip-hop lineage to create something exceptionally solid.
Richie Rich got on the album by what he calls a fluke. LaRussell posted a snippet of a song, “What We Doin,” Richie Rich couldn’t resist and commented his first four bars:
“What we doin? Pulling off slow from the curb. What we doin? Smokin on herb that’s superb..”
LaRussell texted him, “You trynna get on this?”
Richie Rich responded,
“I said—do you think you could reintroduce me to the world? He said, ‘That’s exactly what I’m gonna do. I got you,’” said Richie Rich.
I hopped on a call with Richie Rich in late July to hear more about the album, his music, and his reintroduction to hip hop.
What do you think is special about this album?
LaRussell doesn’t need the assistance of anybody else, but he’s not greedy like that. Instead, he’s reaching back. He’s grabbing people from his era. He’s incorporating people from my era. And that’s just it’s, to me, it’s like strengthening the hip hop vine. Because basically, LaRussell to reach back and grab me is like LaRussell reached back and grabbed the Sugar Hill gang. You know? And they were artists in the 70s, ‘79, ‘78— I believe. A lot of people weren’t even born back then. So it’s just a breath of fresh air to see a kid who’s comfortable enough in his own skin to be able to include people from before him, you know, with him, after him.
What’s changed the most since hip-hop’s inception, for you?
When I first started –I didn’t aspire to be a rapper. I kind of just ended up rapping. But I came from an era where individuality was — was everything, like when you heard a record, you could hear who it was. It was okay to be yourself. What I saw that started changing for me in the music in the late 90s they seemed like they were taking away the individual and replacing that with, you know, everybody sounding alike or acting alike or having one look.
I saw music get to a point where it got away from the ear, and it seemed like it became more from the eye, like it had a look to it, you know what I mean? So a lot of people fell to the wayside. The more talented people who actually had something to say were seeming like they were being pushed out, and what was being ushered in with this, this baller type of image, this baller, rapper. The guy with the most money, the guy with the biggest car. I was always a floss rapper, too. You know, it was all because Oakland has always been about car culture. But when I came up in the music, you know, we were all hustling, so we were just telling the stories about the shit we was doing and putting the money back on the sidewalk.
What’s stayed the same, for you?
The thing that stayed the same, as far as my creation process, is I’m just a truth teller. You know? People, they say I’m a game spitter, but really, all I’m doing is telling the truth, you know? And one thing about telling the truth, it can work and it cannot work. People can love it and some people can hate it, you know? But the thing about the truth is, it never gets old, so my style has stayed the same.
How does this album pay homage to the foundational pillars of hip-hop?
Now it’s, it’s starting to get back to the individualisms. You got a kid like LaRussell, you got a kid like Malachi, who, they don’t wear any jewelry, none at all. You know what I mean. They come out the house with the house shoes on whatever, crocs, you know, sweatpants, just lounging. And once again, the music is coming through the ear, the message is coming through the ear, and then what people are seeing with their eyes is individuality, and it’s working.
You said your son had a strong reaction to the song, can you explain?
My son is a big Mali fan. So, I mean, by doing this record with him, it even made me cool to my son. I’m like, I’m cool now? Because I’m on a record with Mali? He’s 13. So I was like, so prior to that, I wasn’t cool? Like, dude, I have played records for you that I did with 2pac and, you know, and none of that resonated? None of that resonated with him, but when he heard I was on a record with Mali, it was just like - it was a whole different level.
What do you like about LaRussell’s music that stands out to you?
One thing I’ve never been able to do Kayla is be hella fun in my records. I’ve tried to, I’ve had some cool, fun records, but on accident. But like, everything he makes it fun. And he’s still saying a lot of the same shit that I’ve been talking about, but he has a way to make it fun. He has the knees to support those dances he does. My knees ain’t gonna support that!
What has been people’s response to the album?
Four days, 1.2 million views on a 12 bar post is ridiculous. Like my shit hit one 1.2 million. I picked up 12,000 followers off of that play, 12,000 people started following me, and my page has been hopping ever since, like with people talking about: what’s up with the music? what’s up with the music? I know he couldn’t have turned me on to people that are in my normal fan base. These have to be younger people. These are people who are finding out about me from other places, and then they’re tying it together, because they’re in the comments like: this is the dude who has a video with the orange motorcycle, my dad used to watch this. And then somebody’s like, Oh no, that’s the dude that did that song.
What’s next?
We kind of just getting started as far as collabing on shit, because whenever he need me to do something, I’m there, you know? I really want to work with the kid Mali, too. I like Mali style. I like a lot of the shit that he says in his raps, and it’s a fresh air. It’s a brother fresh air for me to hear kids saying some shit again.
