5. CO-LLAB Choir
“So much of music is philosophical and spiritual, especially with the voice, in particular, because the voice is inside your body. It has everything to do with how you think about yourself, how do you take care of yourself? How do you love yourself? How do you show up for yourself?”
— Cava, CO-LLAB Founder
LaRussell has been doing shows at the Empress Theatre for years now. And every time, the team grows bigger: more merch, more instrumentalists, more keys, more DJs. Just when I thought I had seen every iteration of the Good Compenny ensemble, I walked through the hallways of the Empress Theatre and saw a full choir onstage.
After soundcheck, seven members of CO-LLAB choir were huddled in the second green room, scattered between the floor, vanity, stools and the occasional chair, waiting for showtime. They hadn’t had much time to practice, and this would be their first show with LaRussell, the 2025 Empress series. The energy in the room was high, full of nerves, excitement, and joy. LaRussell had found the choir through Rexx Life Raj and Rob Woods and reached out to invite them to rehearse with him. Now, they’re at their first show.
“His energy is just so good and so refreshing,” said choir director Cava.
“Contagious!” another member chimed in.
Now, it was time to see how their voices would elevate his sound live.
When the show began, the choir staggered into two rows. Cava stands angled, facing toward the group, back turned to the crowd, guiding them with subtle, and not-so-subtle, cues: dropping down to signal a volume dip, widening her eyes for intensity, her hands moving with the pace of the song. What might go unnoticed to the audience is just how much she’s communicating with her eyes, and how attuned the choir members are, constantly reading one another for tiny inclinations that tell them to adjust here or there.
There’s an indescribable emotion that comes over you when more than a dozen voices converge at a single moment. Especially when it’s on the iconic background vocals of Overnight Celebrity, their harmonies rising and falling like hip-hop angles behind Twista’s rapid-fire delivery.
“We’re, like, painting with sound in the sky,” Cava says.
The CO-LLAB Choir was founded in 2022 by Cava, an Oakland-based teacher, director, painter, singer, pianist, and more. As a teacher at Oakland School for the Arts, many choir members are her former students. And while not all are from Oakland, their hub is there. The choir is made up of people from different backgrounds, some classically trained, others self-taught. What mattered most wasn’t their resume, it was what they brought to the group: authenticity, community, and voice.
“I look for heart first,” Cava said. “What is your heart like? Do you sing from your heart? Do you understand what it means to express music from this place that’s bigger than all of us?”
Their heart was matched by an undeniable Bay Area flare, a rhythm, a bounce, an attitude that came from being rooted in the soil.
“There’s a really unique vibration in Oakland that I don’t think exists in a lot of other places. It’s very authentic, very concentrated talent, gifts that are just here,” said choir member Zoe.
The Empress show quickly turned into many shows, including the Pergola, All Star Weekend and Sunday Service, with their sound evolving every time. Each performance became an opportunity to learn. While Cava is used to performing with a set list and a consistent band, LaRussell’s shows are entirely improvised. The songs they rehearse might not get played. The band lineup might change night to night. The result?
CAVA: “We’re getting stronger. We’re getting tighter. The rapport between everybody, it’s building confidence, for sure. It’s building our joy as a group. And I think it’s building our flexibility.”
ZOE: “It’s an artist’s dream culture. I feel like we all have this innate design, this authentic sound or pattern to each other. And when you have the space to just freely flow, that’s an artist’s dream. That’s where the divine lives. That’s where the magic is made, in the moment. The more present you are, the more you trust in your skills.”
B DEVEAUX: “That’s where the real fire is. The real magic of music, just having people in the audience, and feeling it as it happens.”
CANDACE GOODWIN: “To be able to improvise, especially as a choir, it’s one thing to do that as an individual artist, but when we can do that together as a collective? It’s really beautiful. It’s cool.”
SKYLR: “It’s sharpening, too. Sometimes something just gets thrown on the setlist and we have to catch it. We haven’t rehearsed it together, and we’ve got to come up with parts on the fly. It’s cool, it makes us better artists.”
Cava agrees. The improvisational nature of the shows keeps her on her toes as a director, and pushes her to grow as an artist. As a pianist, she also gets to learn from Clay and Simon, whom she described as symbiotic twins, seamlessly in sync.
Their process of preparing for LaRussell shows begun with listening, simply absorbing the music. Then comes the mapping: where do harmonies make sense? Where can vocal textures elevate the sound? Some songs are filled with layered harmonies; others just double the lead.
Once the arrangement takes shape, the fine-tuning begins. For CO-LLAB, this process happens over weeks of trial and discovery. They work on blending voices, playing with inflections and intonations to convey emotion. They navigate highs and lows, bounce and feel, tone and delivery, all the subtle choices that shape a song’s final form.
Ideas fly:
“Add a soprano here.”
“What if we echoed this line?”
Piece by piece, it comes together.
The finished product is a reflection of deep teamwork, each member offering what another lacks, and vice versa.
“It’s work,” Cava says, “but it’s joyful work. Fun work. I feel like it’s strengthening us as a group, and just building our relationship with him, it’s been so beautiful.”
The choir has become more than a space to sharpen artistic talent, it’s been a refuge. Often described as a family, the collective offers a creative hub where people from all walks of life can feel safe, seen, and free to share.
“These are kind of hard times to be alive,” said B DeVeaux. “A lot of people are struggling. Folks need healing. Folks need ways to feel and speak about things when there’s not even the right language to hold what we’re going through. And for us, this space has been a healing thing, just the ability to come together.”
The group supports each other both inside and outside of choir, collaborating on projects, forming deep bonds, and tending to each other’s spiritual and creative well-being. Members are deeply multifaceted, covering everything from design and visual art to music production and education.
Zoe spoke openly about what CO-LLAB has meant to them, especially in contrast to the space they left behind. After moving to L.A. to pursue what they thought was an artist’s dream, a production company offering in-house videographers, photographers, engineers, and a state-of-the-art studio, they quickly realized the reality was very different. What was pitched as a creative haven turned out to be a system of abuse. After leaving, Zoe came to understand that the environment was a cult, one in which she experienced multiple forms of abuse.
Joining CO-LLAB Choir was a turning point. It gave them a chance to rewrite their relationship with music and rediscover the joy in their art. It allowed them to remember the goodness still present in the world, even as they continued healing from her past.
“It’s not about what happens to you,” Zoe said. “It’s about how you handle it, how you heal from it, how you unpack it. Life continues, and it’s beautiful.”
